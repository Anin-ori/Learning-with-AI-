# AI Learning Companion — Requirements

Oct 2, 2026 · @anin

## Background

General AI chat tools explain single points well but fail at sustained teaching. Even frontier models can't hold a long-range plan, misjudge what matters for a learner, and drift or hallucinate over long tasks.

They also create a closed "miniature world": the learner ends up studying the model's private version of a subject. When the learner realizes this, trust in everything learned there collapses at once.

Existing AI teaching products don't fill the gap. Most are narrow, built for K-12 schools, or poorly reviewed by learners. The goal is open-source software that makes AI genuinely useful for self-learners.

## Target learners

The first users are self-directed learners of foundational, well-established content rather than cutting-edge topics. The author belongs to this group and is the first user. Motivation is the learner's own: they are free to learn as they choose, and the tool doesn't try to remove the effort learning takes.

## Core principles

1. **Human paths anchor the plan.** AI may plan, but its planning is anchored to paths people have already organized, so its quality stays controllable.
2. **Stay anchored in the shared world.** Every step points to real, existing sources, so the learner is never sealed inside an AI-made world.
3. **AI involvement is graded.** How much the AI teaches depends on the subject and the content itself.
4. **AI must earn its place.** AI is used for two reasons: efficiency (the learner gets there faster) and specificity (it fits this learner's gaps and goal). Where it gives neither, it is a gimmick.
5. **The learner chooses; AI suggests.** Learning is point-based, not a fixed sequence. Brightness shows only learning status: learned (brightest), can learn now (everything it needs is learned), and not reachable yet (darkest). The AI's suggested route toward the learner's goal is a separate mark: a red outer ring on every route topic. The AI's top picks glow white, and the learner can switch the glow off. The learner decides what to study, and dark points stay open; the AI gives suggestions, not results.
6. **The AI creates; we guide.** We don't preset content, slots or quantities for the AI to fill. We tell it what each part is for and what good looks like, and it judges the rest: how to teach, whether an extension is worth writing, how many exercises, tests, hints or notes a case needs, how long an answer should be. A fixed count turns a purpose into a quota, and the AI meets the quota instead of the purpose. Numbers appear only in code, as generous safety limits on run time and page size, never as targets. Checks hold the work to its purpose (R54), and they don't count it.

## Working agreement

Product and teaching decisions stay with the author. Technical decisions are made by Claude, favoring the simplest implementation that works; the author points out problems and judges the results.

## Main direction (confirmed)

A learning session starts in this order:

1. **R4. The learner speaks first.** The learner clearly describes what they need to learn and what their goal is.
2. **R25. Trial unit, if the learner has a foundation.** The learner describes their current situation and takes a trial teaching unit (see below).
3. **R2. Search first.** The AI finds learning paths humans have already organized: open courses, books, and beginner-friendly academic works in the field. Search first means existing human content takes priority. The AI may modify it only to a limited extent; the original and the changes are always shown, and any part the AI modified comes with answers learners can check. The paths found are shown as a visual map (R24), and the learner chooses.
4. **R5. The AI states how far it can guide.** After the trial unit and the path search, and before teaching begins, the AI says how far it can guide this learner, based on the evidence from those steps.

Throughout:

- **R1. Basic content first.** Focus on foundational subjects, where good human-organized material is plentiful.
- **R3. AI planning stays controllable.** AI may plan, but the human-organized path is the anchor that keeps its planning quality in check.

### Opening by learner background

The opening differs depending on whether the learner already has a foundation in the field.

- **R24. No foundation: show the paths.** The AI presents the existing paths in this direction visually, with text and images, matched to the learner's requirements. The learner chooses. The map compares paths in terms a beginner can judge: where each path leads, how long it takes, what it assumes the learner knows, and its style, such as theory-first or project-first. It also highlights what all the paths share, as a human-made signal of what's essential. Open and paid materials are presented objectively side by side, with cost and license stated, and the learner chooses which to use.
- **R25. Some foundation: trial unit first.** The learner describes their current learning situation. The AI then runs a special trial teaching unit to assess them, followed by the same presentation of paths and the learner's choice. The trial unit's material comes from human sources, like all practice material (R2, R20). The AI picks three tasks from a pool of real course problems and shows them directly in the page, summarized in its own words with the original linked and example runs the learner can check against.

### AI path planning (v0.1)

The AI plans flexibly across human materials, and the page keeps that planning controllable.

- **R41. Plans are built from real units only.** The AI composes 2 or 3 plans for the learner's goal (fastest to the goal, strong foundation, project first) from units of verified sources. It never invents a step. Each topic comes from whichever source teaches it best for the learner, and at least two of the three plans combine sources.
- **R42. The page checks every plan in code.** Every step must exist, the shared core must be covered unless the learner already knows it, and the topic order mustn't go against every real course that teaches both topics, so any order a real course uses is accepted. Source switches are counted. Failed checks are shown, never hidden.
- **R43. A single course stays available as a baseline**, so AI plans can be compared with traditional learning.
- **R44. Learners can add their own materials** in three ways. Free online books are fetched and checked at the source, with nothing to provide. Learners can upload their own copy (PDF, EPUB, text or HTML), read in the browser, so lessons are written from the real chapters; the book's text stays in the learner's browser and only the chapter list is saved. Or they just type the title: two models recall the chapters separately, and the page uses the list only if they agree. Learners are never asked for anything the page already has or can get itself. Paid books are never fetched from unofficial copies; only the publisher's official table of contents may be fetched. The AI marks each chapter as able to replace a step, better later, or not fitting now. The page enforces topic and level fit, every change is shown before and after, and the learner decides.
- **R45. Replanning.** After level 1 checks, the AI can propose a new path for the remaining steps, shown as a before-and-after the learner accepts or rejects.

### Lesson quality (v0.3)

AI lessons must be complete, not summaries. Completeness is judged against the source, not the AI's opinion.

- **R46. Lessons are written against the chapter's checked outline.** The outline lists each item's key points, checked at the source, and what the chapter's own practice questions and programs require. Writers must teach every item and key point in order, at the chapter's depth, using only what the learner has covered or explaining anything new. They must teach what the practice needs without writing the practice itself. Key points describe ideas in the page's own words rather than quoting the book's examples or practice questions.
- **R47. A team of agents builds every lesson.** A planner splits the chapter into parts, in the chapter's order (usually 3; a complete part matters more than the number, and long text is fine). For every part, at the same time, a writer drafts it. A source checker (coverage and accuracy) and a learner checker (accuracy, prerequisites judged against this learner, clarity, rule breaks) review it independently. A lead merges both reports, and an editor fixes what blocks. A final checker then reads the whole lesson.
- **R48. A fixed bar decides what blocks delivery:** an outline item missing or too thin to learn from; a factual error or a wrong stated output; a feature this learner doesn't know, used without explanation; a rule break (AI-written exercises, copied book text); or a passage this learner would clearly misunderstand. Everything else is advice, shown under the lesson, and never blocks. Missing exercises are never a problem. In AI-led lessons, a rule counts as taught only when an example shows it changing a result. Reusing the book's own examples, or answering the chapter's own practice questions, is a rule break.
- **R49. Practice pointers come from verified source data,** written by the page. The AI never names exercises itself. After the last part, the page shows a practice section: how many questions and programs the chapter has, a preview of what they ask in the page's own words, a link straight to the questions, and a link to the book's own answers when it has them (Automate the Boring Stuff: Appendix B). The questions themselves are never reproduced or rewritten.
- **R50. Triage before fixing.** A problem that editing a few passages can't fix is core, and only that part is rewritten. Anything else is fixed with targeted edits, not regenerated.
- **R51. Only a fully compliant version is delivered.** The learner sees a lesson only after it passes the final check. If it doesn't pass within 3 final rounds, nothing new is delivered and the previous lesson stays. Lessons made by the old single-model process are not shown.
- **R52. The team's work is visible and recorded.** While it runs, the learner sees each agent's progress by part. Afterwards they can see what was fixed before delivery and the advice that didn't block. Every run's result and reasons go into the test log. The learner starts each build, since a full lesson takes about 15 to 20 requests.
- **R53. The learner chooses how a lesson is built.** Whole chapter at once: all parts are written in parallel, one final check reads the whole lesson, and the learner reads it when everything is ready. Part by part: the whole chapter is planned first, then each part is built and checked against the parts already delivered, and the learner builds the next part when ready. Each delivered part must pass its own final check, and the last part's check covers the whole chapter's practice needs.
- **R54. The AI creates; the framework guides.** A standing design rule from the author: "we want the AI to create, and what we do is guide and constrain its creation", not preset content or slots for it to fill. Since v1.14 the teaching standards are one short guide that the planner, teacher and checker all read. It covers depth (understanding the learner could rebuild), surprises (worth showing only when explaining them reveals how the subject works, as in Fluent Python's `t[2] += [50, 60]` on a tuple; slips teach nothing, and no mistake is called common), honest scope (say where a rule holds), extensions (R55) and new things (explain briefly where they appear). The guide holds for every subject; a subject profile supplies only illustrations, never content to reuse. The planner decides the approach and structure, and the teacher may improve on the plan. The checker is an editor: errors block delivery until fixed, and shortfalls against the guide go back to the teacher once, then stay visible as advice (R48).
- **R55. Extensions go down, not sideways.** Part of the guide (R54), for every subject. The most valuable extension after a lesson explains what happens underneath: the steps the language actually takes, what the feature is built on, why it behaves the way it does, and how the learner can observe it for themselves (in Python: `dis` for bytecode, `type()`, `id()` and special methods for objects). A tour of more uses or later features adds only breadth, which later lessons cover anyway. An extension is explained properly or left out: concrete and exact, never a passing hint such as "a clearer way comes later". Under the hood, the AI is exact about what it knows, says plainly where its certainty ends, and says where to check. The learner doesn't have to master it; the page shows extensions after the lesson in their own box marked optional, and whether to write one is the AI's judgement, not a slot. The tutor answers "how does it work" questions the same way.
- **R56. Practice comes in sets, once what has been learned adds up to real tasks.** A framework standard for every subject. Practice on one narrow point is a drill that repeats the lesson; practice is worth doing when learned points combine into a small real task where the learner must decide how to fit them together. Practice is its own section, separate from Learn. First an AI judges, from what the learner has learned and what their lessons taught, whether practice is worth doing now; if not, it says why and which points would change that, and the learner may build a set anyway. A designer plans the set (how many exercises, easier to harder, combining recent points and bringing earlier ones back as review, is its judgement under the practice standards, not a fixed count), and then each exercise is written in full by its own request: a complete task with a worked example, the decision it makes the learner work out, a reference solution using only learned material (or explaining briefly anything new), tests and hints that never give code. The page runs every reference solution against its tests (Python in the browser). An editor then separates errors (the learner couldn't fairly pass it, or it is a drill rather than practice) from improvements; only exercises with something to fix are rewritten, errors for up to 2 rounds and improvements once. An exercise that still has errors is left out and named on the set; the set fails only if every exercise does. The editor's remaining suggestions are shown with the set. The learner writes code in the page and checks it against the same tests; hints and the reference solution are opt-in.
- **R57. Notes live on the map, and questions are kept with their point.** The Notes tab opens the knowledge map in notes mode. Topics with notes stay lit and the rest dim; the overview pane becomes an index of the notes and recent questions; a topic's point list marks notes and questions; and a point's details show its notes and questions first. Every question asked to the tutor is saved with its answer under that point, shown in its notes, and loaded back into the tutor chat when the point is opened again. The list view stays one click away, for testing yourself with hidden key terms.

## AI involvement levels (confirmed)

Who teaches is not fixed. How much the AI teaches depends on whether it can handle the content.

- **R15. Graded AI involvement.** The level of AI participation is set by subject and by content, and can differ even within one module. For now, the grade is assigned by multiple AIs cross-checking, constrained by search-first (R2). The author will judge the results in the test version. In the Learn step, the grade sets the teaching mode: 60% or more gives an AI-led lesson with the source for depth and practice, 30 to 59% gives guided reading, and below 30% the learner studies the source with AI context. Lessons can be cross-checked by two more models.

| Example subject | Share the AI can explain | AI's role | What humans provide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Programming | About 80% | Main explainer | Outline and core resources |
| Philosophy | Under 10% | Provides resources that broaden perspective; helps look up concepts | Original philosophical texts; core thinking is left to the learner (method to be detailed later) |

## Verifying the teaching and self-checking (confirmed)

During learning, the tool verifies the AI's teaching; checking understanding is the learner's own responsibility, supported by practice sets in their own section (R56).

- **R16. The AI doesn't test the learner.** The learner is not quizzed or tested by the AI while working through the path.
- **R17. Cross-verify what the AI teaches.** The AI's teaching is verified by cross-checking, with multiple AIs. A learner with only one model gets several independent runs of that model plus comparison with human sources, and the check is labeled as weaker.
- **R18. Practice for self-testing.** The learner gets practice to test themselves, in its own section, in sets rather than after each point (R56). Self-checking is the learner's responsibility.
- **R19. Learners can ask the AI about practice.** Learners can bring practice questions to the AI directly; answering questions is what AI does best.
- **R20. Practice is written by AI under constraints, and proven before it is shown.** Changed Oct 4, 2026 (v1.11) by the author's decision: "we want the AI to create; what we do is guide and constrain its creation." Until then, practice came only from human sources and the AI never invented questions, because the author had tested AI-made questions and found the results a disaster. That history is why every set is now judged for readiness, held to the practice standards by a checker, and run before delivery (R56). Human sources stay as the calibration for scale and style, not as a menu.
- **R21. AI answers are labeled as fallible.** Answers the AI writes, including those for AI-modified parts, are cross-checked with multiple AIs and state that AI isn't necessarily correct. Each answer also shows whether the AIs agreed or gave different answers, so the learner knows where to be careful. Judging them is the learner's responsibility.
- **R39. Review lives in the exercises.** Review is built into the practice exercises, and learners review on their own. The AI doesn't schedule reviews; it follows the designed teaching plan.
- **R40. The plan and the learner's place are saved.** Between sessions, the tool keeps only the chosen teaching plan and where the learner is on it.

## Goal projects (confirmed)

At the start of teaching, the AI presets several real projects the learner could complete in the field once the current content is learned. They exist to test the teaching, not to drive it.

- **R6. Preset from the learner's goal.** Projects are set at the start of teaching, based on the goal the learner stated. They come in three levels, described below.
- **R7. AI use clearly labeled.** Each project is marked as independent or AI-allowed, according to its level.
- **R8. Not the core of teaching.** The human teaching path stays the core. Teaching toward the projects causes serious overfitting, which the author has observed in testing.
- **R9. They check the teaching, not the learner.** The projects let the AI evaluate whether its teaching was adequate.
- **R10. Coverage check first.** Before presenting the no-AI projects, the AI assesses whether its teaching fully covered the knowledge they require.
- **R11. Presented after teaching.** Once teaching ends, the AI presents the projects and marks which allow AI and which must be done independently.
- **R12. Examiner separate from teacher.** The part of the system that teaches cannot see the projects. A separate step holds them and runs the coverage check.
- **R13. Fill gaps from the human path.** A gap found by the coverage check is filled from the relevant human-made material, never by teaching to the project. If no human material covers it, the project is swapped or postponed.
- **R14. Projects come from the human world.** Projects are drawn from course capstones, textbook problem sets, and real practitioner work, never invented by the AI. The AI may adapt them within the limits in R2.

### Project levels

Three levels, kept deliberately simple, rising from a visible record of what was learned to work at a real-world standard.

| Level | Content | AI use | How it's judged |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Basic knowledge check | Chapter notes: the chapter's key points on one page, checked at the source and written by the page, that the learner keeps and comes back to. No questions. The learner can hide the key terms as blanks and click each one to reveal it. Each section links to the lesson part that teaches it, and the short examples are our own, run in Python. Not difficult; the focus is letting learners clearly see what they've learned. Steps without checked key points fall back to listing key points. | Not allowed | Against the source material |
| 2. Comprehensive practical test | Opinions on more challenging or controversial topics, or beginner-level projects. | Not allowed. Learners may use their own notes and the course materials, but no internet access | Opinions are judged on reasoning and evidence, not on the position taken |
| 3. Complex real work | The most complex content, requiring learners to go beyond surface-level knowledge. | Freely allowed | Work at least at a basic commercial standard; arguments at a basic academic level. Compared against real human-made examples found by search |

### When a learner struggles with a project

The tool first checks its own teaching, then fixes it with focused teaching, and only then hands the problem back to the learner.

1. **R35. Check the teaching first.** When a learner can't complete a no-AI project, multiple AIs check whether the teaching for it was up to standard.
2. **R36. Breakthrough teaching on the gap.** If it wasn't, the AI gives focused breakthrough teaching on that specific area, then re-evaluates, repeating until the teaching is good enough to solve the preset problems. The examiner passes the teacher only the area to strengthen, never the project itself (R12), and the material comes from human sources (R13).
3. **R37. A cap on rounds.** The loop runs a limited number of rounds. If the teaching still falls short, the tool says so plainly and points the learner to human resources.
4. **R38. Otherwise, the learner corrects themselves.** If the teaching was up to standard, the learner works on correcting their own learning first.

## Model integration (contingency plan)

Learners bring their own model; the software provides only the teaching framework and principles. Details are set aside for later.

- **R22. Bring your own model.** The software leaves an API interface where each learner chooses and pays for their own model.
- **R23. The software is the framework.** The software provides the teaching operation framework and principles, not the model itself.

## Search layer (confirmed)

Search belongs to the framework, not the model, so every learner works from the same retrieved material whatever model they use. The design is not tied to any one vendor.

- **R26. Query known high-quality sources directly.** The framework itself searches open textbook libraries, university course archives, encyclopedias for concepts, and open-access repositories for beginner-friendly academic works.
- **R27. Share and reuse verified paths.** Paths found and verified for a subject are cached. The project maintains a community index of verified paths for common subjects. In the first version this is a local cache; the shared community index comes later.
- **R28. The framework verifies sources.** Whatever found a source, the framework opens its link and confirms it exists and matches what's claimed.
- **R29. Pluggable general search as fallback.** For anything the known sources don't cover, the learner can plug in a web search provider: a commercial search API with their own key, a self-hosted metasearch engine such as SearXNG, or a model's built-in search.
- **R30. Optional split roles.** Learners may use a strong-search model for the search step and a different model for teaching. Later phase.

### Restricted internet access

The first version handles restricted access with four simple measures.

- **R31. Unreachable is not missing.** Verification tells "doesn't exist" apart from "can't be reached from here," and the path map marks unreachable materials instead of hiding or rejecting them.
- **R32. Sources and search are configurable.** The source list and search engines live in an editable configuration, so learners or the community can add sources reachable in their region.
- **R33. Any compatible model endpoint.** The model interface accepts any endpoint that follows the common OpenAI-style API format, so regional providers and local models work without extra effort.
- **R34. Keep what's been found.** Found paths and downloaded open materials are cached locally, so they keep working if access drops.

Later, if needed: a downloadable community index with mirrors, and a full offline mode.

## Deferred details (revisit later)

Set aside to focus on the main direction first. These are Claude's suggestions; keep, change, or drop each one when we come back to details.

- **P1. Verify every source (adopted as R28).** Each recommended course or book must be confirmed to exist and come with a link. Nothing unverified is shown, which guards against invented references.
- **P2. Compare several paths (adopted in R24).** Topics that most curricula share form the core; topics found in only one are optional. Agreement among human curricula answers "what matters."
- **P3. Prefer open materials (superseded by R24: present objectively, learner chooses).** Favor freely available sources such as OpenStax, MIT OpenCourseWare, and LibreTexts so anyone can follow the path. Paid books are pointed to, never reproduced.
- **P4. The learner studies the real materials (superseded by R15).** The AI explains, quizzes, and gives feedback around them rather than replacing them.
- **P5. Keep progress outside the chat (superseded by R39–R40).** A persistent record shows what has been covered on the path and how well, topic by topic.
- **P6. Check before moving on.** Superseded by R16 and R18, since self-checking is the learner's responsibility. Originally, progress was to be gated by questions or exercises, not by the learner saying "got it."
