Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Differentiation: This part covers the derivative as a limit of difference quotients. Differentiability implies continuity, but not the converse (|x|, and a nowhere-differentiable example mentioned). Proofs of the sum, product, quotient and chain rules, and the derivative of an inverse function. Local extrema and Fermat's theorem, Rolle's theorem, the mean value theorem and the Cauchy mean value theorem, and their consequences: zero derivative means constant, the sign of the derivative and monotonicity, and Lipschitz bounds from a bounded derivative. Darboux's theorem (derivatives have the intermediate value property even when not continuous, with x^2 sin(1/x) as the standard example of a discontinuous derivative). L'Hopital's rule with proof, higher derivatives, and Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder, used to estimate errors. Convex functions may be touched briefly. Edges: Taylor series and the question of when a function equals its Taylor series belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The integral form of the remainder and the fundamental theorem of calculus belong to 'The Riemann integral'. Limits and continuity come from the previous part and are not redone. What matters for this learner: the mean value theorem is the main tool here, and the learner should be able to use it to prove inequalities and properties of functions. Differentiation technique as a drill is out of scope.
  - The derivative and its rules (your part): This part covers the derivative of a function at a point as the limit of the difference quotient (equivalently, of (f(x) - f(c))/(x - c) as x tends to c), one-sided derivatives, and the derivative as a function on an interval. It proves that differentiability implies continuity and shows the converse fails: |x| at 0, x^(1/3) with a vertical tangent, and a continuous nowhere-differentiable function (Weierstrass) mentioned only, with its construction left to 'Sequences and series of functions'. It proves the sum, constant multiple, product and quotient rules, the chain rule (done carefully, avoiding the faulty proof that divides by g(x) - g(c), for example via the Caratheodory form f(x) - f(c) = phi(x)(x - c) with phi continuous at c), and the derivative of an inverse function for a continuous strictly monotone function with nonzero derivative (for example n-th roots). Standard examples include x^n by induction, x^2 sin(1/x) as a function differentiable everywhere whose derivative is not continuous at 0, and x sin(1/x) as continuous but not differentiable at 0. Edges: limits of functions, the sequential criterion and continuity of inverses of strictly monotone functions come from 'Limits of functions and continuity' and are used, not reproved. Higher derivatives are introduced in 'L'Hopital's rule and Taylor's theorem'. Local extrema and the mean value theorem belong to 'The mean value theorem and its consequences'. Derivatives of exp, log, sin and cos are used with their familiar values; they are proved in 'Sequences and series of functions'. No differentiation drills. What matters for this learner: computing derivatives from the definition with epsilon-delta or limit-law arguments, writing the rule proofs themselves, and knowing why the naive chain-rule proof fails.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- The mean value theorem and its consequences: This part covers local maxima and minima and Fermat's theorem (an interior local extremum of a differentiable function has zero derivative), and why endpoints and non-differentiable points must be checked separately. Then Rolle's theorem, the mean value theorem and the Cauchy (generalised) mean value theorem, with proofs that use the extreme value theorem. Consequences: a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant (and two functions with equal derivatives differ by a constant); the sign of the derivative and monotonicity (including why f'(c) > 0 at a single point does not make f increasing near c); a bounded derivative gives a Lipschitz bound |f(x) - f(y)| <= M|x - y|, hence uniform continuity; proving inequalities such as sin x <= x, e^x >= 1 + x and Bernoulli-type bounds; and counting roots of equations with Rolle's theorem. Darboux's theorem: a derivative has the intermediate value property even when it is not continuous, so a derivative cannot have a jump discontinuity, with x^2 sin(1/x) as the standard example of a discontinuous derivative. Edges: the extreme value theorem, Lipschitz functions and uniform continuity come from 'Limits of functions and continuity'. The definition of the derivative, the rules and the example x^2 sin(1/x) come from 'The derivative and its rules'. L'Hopital's rule, higher derivatives, Taylor's theorem and convexity belong to 'L'Hopital's rule and Taylor's theorem', which use the Cauchy mean value theorem proved here. The fundamental theorem of calculus belongs to 'The Riemann integral'. What matters for this learner: the mean value theorem is the main tool of the whole Differentiation part. The learner should be able to choose the right function and interval to apply it to, and use it to prove inequalities, monotonicity, uniqueness of roots and Lipschitz bounds, and should know exactly which hypotheses (continuity on the closed interval, differentiability on the open one) each theorem needs.
- L'Hopital's rule and Taylor's theorem: This part covers L'Hopital's rule for the 0/0 form, with a proof from the Cauchy mean value theorem, and the infinity/infinity form and limits at infinity (the infinity/infinity case may be proved or carefully stated), with examples where the hypotheses fail or where the rule gives no answer even though the limit exists. Higher derivatives, the classes C^n and C-infinity, and the n-th derivative of a product (Leibniz formula) at least stated. Taylor polynomials as the polynomial matching f and its first n derivatives at a point, and Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder, proved by repeated use of Rolle's or the Cauchy mean value theorem. Using the remainder to estimate errors (for example approximating e, sin x or sqrt(1 + x) to a stated accuracy) and to prove inequalities. The second derivative test for local extrema as an application. A brief treatment of convex functions: the definition by chords, the slope (three-chord) inequality, a differentiable function is convex exactly when its derivative is increasing, and f'' >= 0 implies convexity, with Jensen's inequality optional. Edges: Rolle's theorem, the mean value theorem and the Cauchy mean value theorem are proved in 'The mean value theorem and its consequences' and are used here. Taylor series, radius of convergence and the question of when a function equals its Taylor series (e^(-1/x^2)) belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The integral form of the remainder belongs to 'The Riemann integral'. What matters for this learner: L'Hopital's rule is a theorem with hypotheses to check, not a reflex; and Taylor's theorem is a quantitative mean value theorem whose remainder term gives a bound the learner can actually compute and justify.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
