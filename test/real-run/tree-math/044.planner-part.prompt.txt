Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Sequences and series of functions: This part covers pointwise and uniform convergence of sequences of functions, with examples where pointwise limits lose continuity (x^n on [0,1]) or where integrals and limits cannot be swapped. The Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence and the sup-norm view. Theorems on interchanging limits: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous; uniform convergence lets limits pass through integrals; and differentiating term by term needs uniform convergence of the derivatives. Series of functions and the Weierstrass M-test. Power series: the radius of convergence (Cauchy-Hadamard formula with limsup, and the ratio form), uniform convergence on smaller closed intervals, behaviour at the endpoints (Abel's theorem stated), and term-by-term differentiation and integration. Taylor series, and the gap between infinitely differentiable and analytic functions (e^(-1/x^2)). Defining exp, log, sin and cos rigorously by power series and deriving their basic properties, which closes the loop on functions used informally earlier. Mentioned as highlights: a continuous nowhere-differentiable function (Weierstrass) and the Weierstrass approximation theorem, stated. Dini's theorem is optional. Edges: series of numbers and their tests come from 'Series of real numbers'. Taylor's theorem with remainder for one function comes from 'Differentiation' and 'The Riemann integral'. Fourier series are only named in the next part. What matters for this learner: this part shows why rigour was needed all along. The learner should be able to decide whether a convergence is uniform and to justify every swap of a limit with continuity, an integral or a derivative.
  - Interchanging limits and series of functions (your part): This part covers the theorems that justify swapping limits, with full proofs. A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (the epsilon/3 argument), together with the reading of this as interchanging two limits. Uniform convergence on [a,b] lets the limit pass through the Riemann integral, and a uniform limit of integrable functions is integrable. Term-by-term differentiation: if the derivatives converge uniformly and the functions converge at one point, then the functions converge uniformly and the limit is differentiable with the limit derivative. Series of functions as sequences of partial sums, with each theorem restated for series. The Weierstrass M-test with proof, and how to use it in practice. Dini's theorem (monotone convergence of continuous functions on a compact set to a continuous limit is uniform) is optional. Highlight: the Weierstrass continuous nowhere-differentiable function (or a simpler sawtooth variant, as in Abbott), shown continuous by the M-test, with the nowhere-differentiability argument sketched rather than required. Edges: the definitions of pointwise and uniform convergence, the counterexamples, the sup-norm and the Cauchy criterion come from 'Pointwise and uniform convergence'. Power series are the next part and use these theorems; they are not developed here. Numerical series tests come from 'Series of real numbers'. The Riemann integral, its properties and the mean value theorem come from 'The Riemann integral' and 'Differentiation'. What matters for this learner: knowing exactly which hypothesis each interchange theorem needs, being able to justify every swap of a limit with continuity, an integral or a derivative in an exercise, and using the M-test fluently.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Pointwise and uniform convergence: This part covers what it means for a sequence of functions to converge. Pointwise convergence is defined first, then a set of warning examples: x^n on [0,1], whose pointwise limit is discontinuous; a moving or growing 'bump' sequence whose integrals do not tend to the integral of the limit; and a sequence like sin(nx)/sqrt(n), which converges uniformly while its derivatives do not converge. Uniform convergence is defined, and the difference is spelled out at the level of the quantifiers: N may depend on x, or N must work for every x at once. Also covered: the sup-norm view (f_n -> f uniformly exactly when sup|f_n - f| -> 0), practical ways to decide whether a convergence is uniform (find the pointwise limit, then bound or compute the sup, often with calculus, or exhibit points x_n that stay far from the limit), uniform convergence on subsets (for example x^n on [0,b] with b < 1 but not on [0,1)), and the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence with proof. Edges: this part only defines and tests convergence. The theorems about passing limits through continuity, integrals and derivatives, and series of functions with the M-test, belong to 'Interchanging limits and series of functions'. Sequences of numbers, Cauchy sequences and their completeness come from 'Sequences' and are not reproved. What matters for this learner: being able to decide with proof whether a given convergence is uniform, and to negate the uniform convergence statement correctly. The counterexamples here are the reason the next part's theorems need uniform convergence.
- Power series and Taylor series: This part covers series of the form sum a_n (x - c)^n. The radius of convergence via the Cauchy-Hadamard formula with limsup, with the ratio form as a convenient special case; absolute convergence inside the radius and divergence outside. Uniform convergence on every closed interval strictly inside the interval of convergence (via the M-test), hence continuity of the sum. Behaviour at the endpoints, with examples that converge at both, one or neither endpoint, and Abel's theorem stated (with its use for sums like log 2 = 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - ... or pi/4 from arctan). Term-by-term differentiation and integration, including that the differentiated series has the same radius, and that a power series is infinitely differentiable with a_n = f^(n)(c)/n!, so the coefficients are unique. Taylor series of a smooth function, proving a function equals its Taylor series by showing the Taylor remainder tends to zero, and the gap between infinitely differentiable and analytic functions with e^(-1/x^2) (zero at 0) as the standard example. Closing contrast: the Weierstrass approximation theorem, stated, saying every continuous function on [a,b] is a uniform limit of polynomials even when it has no Taylor series. Edges: the interchange theorems and the M-test come from 'Interchanging limits and series of functions' and are applied, not reproved. Limsup and the root and ratio tests come from 'Sequences' and 'Series of real numbers'. Taylor's theorem with remainder (Lagrange and integral forms) comes from 'Differentiation' and 'The Riemann integral'. Defining exp, log, sin and cos is the next part. What matters for this learner: computing a radius of convergence with justification, knowing that convergence is uniform only on smaller closed intervals, and understanding that being smooth does not make a function equal to its Taylor series.
- Defining the elementary functions: This part closes the loop on functions used informally since the start of the course. The exponential is defined as the power series sum x^n/n!, which converges for all x. From the series and the Cauchy product (stated in 'Series of real numbers') it proves exp(x+y) = exp(x)exp(y), exp' = exp, exp > 0, strict monotonicity and the limits at plus and minus infinity, and identifies exp(1) with e = lim (1 + 1/n)^n. The logarithm is defined as the inverse of exp (by the continuity and differentiability of inverse functions), and log(xy) = log x + log y and log' = 1/x are derived. General powers are defined as a^x = exp(x log a), which agrees with rational powers. Sine and cosine are defined by their power series, with sin' = cos, cos' = -sin, sin^2 + cos^2 = 1 and the addition formulas proved (for example by the uniqueness argument for f'' = -f, or from the series). pi is defined as twice the first positive zero of cos, which needs an intermediate value argument, and periodicity follows. Optionally, the alternative route defining log as the integral of 1/t is mentioned. Edges: power series, their radius of convergence, term-by-term differentiation and uniqueness of coefficients come from 'Power series and Taylor series'. Inverse function continuity and differentiability come from 'Limits of functions and continuity' and 'Differentiation'. The mean value theorem consequences (zero derivative means constant) come from 'Differentiation'. Complex exponentials and Euler's formula are only named as a pointer to 'Where analysis leads'. What matters for this learner: seeing that every familiar property of these functions can now be proved from a definition, and practising the typical technique of proving an identity by showing two functions satisfy the same differential equation with the same initial value.
