Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Limits of functions and continuity (your part): This part covers limits of functions on subsets of the real line: the epsilon-delta definition at limit points of the domain, one-sided limits, limits at infinity and infinite limits, the sequential criterion linking functional limits to 'Sequences', and the limit laws via that criterion. Continuity: the epsilon-delta and sequential definitions, the algebra and composition of continuous functions, continuity of polynomials and rational functions, and the classification of discontinuities with examples (Dirichlet function, Thomae's function, sin(1/x), x sin(1/x)). Main theorems: continuous images of compact sets are compact, the extreme value theorem, the intermediate value theorem (via connectedness, or via the supremum), uniform continuity and the Heine-Cantor theorem (a continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous), and Lipschitz functions. Monotone functions: their one-sided limits, the countability of their discontinuities, and the continuity of inverses of continuous strictly monotone functions (for example n-th roots). Edges: compactness and connectedness are defined in 'Topology of the real line'. Derivatives belong to 'Differentiation'. Continuity of power series and uniform limits belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. What matters for this learner: writing epsilon-delta proofs where delta depends on epsilon and the point, understanding the difference between continuity and uniform continuity at the level of the quantifiers, and knowing which hypothesis each big theorem needs.

Piece: Limits of functions
Topic t20: Limits of functions
  p247 | The epsilon-delta definition of a functional limit | State lim_{x->c} f(x) = L for f: A -> R with 0 < |x - c| < delta and x in A, and explain why c must be a limit point of A (otherwise every L would qualify and the limit would not be unique) and why the value f(c), or whether f is even defined at c, plays no role. | needs: nothing
  p248 | Proving a limit from the definition: scratch work then proof | Find a delta by working backwards from |f(x) - L| < epsilon (scratch work) and then write the forward proof cleanly, for linear functions and for x^2, using a preliminary bound such as delta <= 1 and delta = min(1, ...) to control the extra factor. | needs: p247
  p249 | How delta depends on epsilon and the point | Prove limits such as 1/x at c > 0 and sqrt x at c > 0 (and sqrt x at 0 from the right side of the domain), and see from the formulas for delta that it must shrink as c approaches a trouble spot, not only as epsilon shrinks. | needs: p248
  p250 | Negating the limit definition | Write out 'f(x) does not tend to L as x -> c' (there is an epsilon such that every delta has some x in A with 0 < |x - c| < delta and |f(x) - L| >= epsilon) and 'the limit does not exist', and use it to show directly that a given L is not the limit. | needs: p247
  p251 | The sequential criterion for functional limits | Prove that lim_{x->c} f(x) = L if and only if f(x_n) -> L for every sequence (x_n) in A with x_n != c and x_n -> c, the converse by contrapositive choosing x_n within delta = 1/n. | needs: p247, p250
  p252 | Uniqueness and the algebraic limit laws for functions | Use the sequential criterion to prove that a functional limit is unique and that limits of sums, scalar multiples, products and quotients (with nonzero limit in the denominator) behave as expected, without redoing any epsilon-delta work. | needs: p251
  p253 | Order and squeeze for functional limits | Use the sequential criterion to prove that limits preserve non-strict inequalities between functions near c and the squeeze theorem for functions, and apply it to examples such as x sin(1/x) -> 0 as x -> 0. | needs: p251
  p254 | Proving a limit does not exist with two sequences | Show that a limit fails to exist by finding sequences x_n -> c and y_n -> c (avoiding c) with f(x_n) and f(y_n) converging to different values or with f(x_n) divergent, as for sin(1/x) at 0 and the sign function at 0. | needs: p251
  p255 | One-sided limits | Define the right-hand and left-hand limits at c (limits of the restriction of f to A intersected with (c, infinity) or (-infinity, c)), and prove that the two-sided limit exists and equals L exactly when both one-sided limits exist and equal L. | needs: p247
  p256 | Limits at infinity | Define lim_{x->infinity} f(x) = L (for every epsilon there is M such that x > M in A gives |f(x) - L| < epsilon), write its negation, prove examples such as 1/x and (2x+1)/(x+3), and note that the sequential criterion and limit laws carry over. | needs: p247
  p257 | Infinite limits | Define lim_{x->c} f(x) = infinity (for every M there is delta with f(x) > M whenever 0 < |x - c| < delta) and the variants as x -> infinity, write their negations, and prove examples such as 1/x^2 -> infinity at 0 and 1/x having no infinite two-sided limit at 0. | needs: p247

Piece: Continuity and types of discontinuity
Topic t22: Continuity and types of discontinuity
  p267 | Continuity at a point and on a set: the epsilon-delta definition | State the definition of f: A -> R being continuous at a point c in A (for every epsilon > 0 there is delta > 0 such that |x - c| < delta and x in A imply |f(x) - f(c)| < epsilon) and of continuity on a set, and explain why c must be in the domain, why f(c) now matters (unlike for limits), and why a restriction of a function can be continuous where the original is not. | needs: nothing
  p268 | Negating continuity | Write out correctly what it means for f to be discontinuous at c (there is an epsilon > 0 such that for every delta > 0 some x in A with |x - c| < delta has |f(x) - f(c)| >= epsilon), and use this negation to prove simple discontinuities, such as a step function at its jump, directly from the definition. | needs: p267
  p269 | Proving continuity from the definition | Write complete epsilon-delta proofs that particular functions (for example 3x + 1, |x|, x^2 and 1/x at a given point) are continuous, doing scratch work to find delta and noticing when delta depends on both epsilon and the point c. | needs: p267
  p270 | Continuity through limits; isolated points | Prove that at a limit point c of the domain, f is continuous at c exactly when the limit of f(x) as x -> c exists and equals f(c), and that every function is automatically continuous at an isolated point of its domain (so every function on the integers is continuous). | needs: p267
  p271 | Sequential characterisation of continuity | Prove that f is continuous at c exactly when f(x_n) -> f(c) for every sequence (x_n) in the domain converging to c, and use this form to move results about sequences over to continuous functions. | needs: p270
  p272 | Proving discontinuity with sequences | Show that f is discontinuous at c by finding one sequence x_n -> c in the domain with f(x_n) not converging to f(c), and recognise when this is easier than negating the epsilon-delta definition. | needs: p271
  p273 | Algebra of continuous functions | Prove that if f and g are continuous at c then so are kf, f + g, fg and, when g(c) is not 0, f/g, by using the sequential characterisation together with the limit laws instead of redoing epsilon work. | needs: p271
  p274 | Composition of continuous functions | Prove that if f is continuous at c and g is continuous at f(c) then g o f is continuous at c, both by chaining the epsilon-delta definitions and by sequences, and see why continuity of g at f(c), not just a limit there, is needed. | needs: p267
  p275 | Continuity of polynomials, rational functions and familiar functions | Deduce that polynomials are continuous on R and rational functions on their domains, and build continuity of more complex expressions (such as sqrt(1 + x^2) or sin(x)/x for x not 0) from familiar continuous functions like exp, sin and sqrt, whose continuity is used now and proved when they are defined rigorously. | needs: p273, p274
  p276 | Topological form of continuity | Prove that f: R -> R is continuous exactly when the preimage of every open set is open (equivalently, of every closed set is closed), state the relative version for functions on a subset A (preimages are open relative to A), and use it to see at once that sets like {x : f(x) > 0} are open. | needs: p267
  p277 | Types of discontinuity: removable, jump, essential | Classify a discontinuity at a limit point c using one-sided limits: removable (the limit exists but differs from f(c) or f(c) is undefined), jump (both one-sided limits exist but differ), and essential (some one-sided limit fails to exist), and identify each type in simple examples. | needs: p270
  p278 | sin(1/x) and x sin(1/x) at 0 | Show that sin(1/x) has an essential discontinuity at 0 however it is defined there, while x sin(1/x) has a removable discontinuity that becomes continuous when the value at 0 is set to 0, using sequences for the first and the squeeze theorem for the second. | needs: p272, p277
  p279 | The Dirichlet function: continuous nowhere | Prove that the function equal to 1 on the rationals and 0 on the irrationals is discontinuous at every point, by choosing sequences of rationals or irrationals converging to the point. | needs: p272
  p280 | x times the Dirichlet function: continuous at exactly one point | Prove that x times the Dirichlet function is continuous at 0 (take delta = epsilon) and discontinuous everywhere else, and see from it that continuity at a single point says nothing about nearby points. | needs: p269, p279
  p281 | Thomae's function: continuous exactly at the irrationals | Prove that the function equal to 1/q at p/q in lowest terms (q > 0) and 0 at irrationals is discontinuous at every rational and continuous at every irrational, using that only finitely many rationals near a point have denominator at most N; this tests the quantifiers in the definition at their hardest. | needs: p269, p279

Piece: The extreme and intermediate value theorems
Topic t23: The extreme and intermediate value theorems
  p282 | Continuous image of a compact set is compact | Prove that if K is compact and f is continuous on K then f(K) is compact, using sequential compactness: take a sequence in f(K), pull it back to K, extract a convergent subsequence, and push it forward by continuity. | needs: nothing
  p283 | The same theorem with open covers | Give the second proof that continuous images of compact sets are compact, by pulling an open cover of f(K) back to an open cover of K and taking a finite subcover, and see why the two proofs agree on R. | needs: nothing
  p284 | The extreme value theorem | Prove that a continuous function on a nonempty compact set, in particular on [a,b], is bounded and attains a maximum and a minimum, and explain this as local continuity becoming global information through compactness. | needs: p282
  p285 | Why every hypothesis of the extreme value theorem is needed | Produce counterexamples when one hypothesis is dropped: x or 1/x on (0,1) (open interval), x on [0, infinity) (unbounded domain), a function on [0,1] with a jump or a pole (discontinuous), and 1/(x^2 - 2) on the rationals in [1,2] (domain in Q, not complete), and say which step of the proof breaks in each case. | needs: p284
  p286 | Continuous image of a connected set is connected | Prove that if E is connected and f is continuous on E then f(E) is connected, by showing that a separation of f(E) would pull back to a separation of E. | needs: nothing
  p287 | The intermediate value theorem via connectedness | State the intermediate value theorem (a continuous f on [a,b] takes every value between f(a) and f(b)) and derive it in a few lines from the connectedness of [a,b] and of its image. | needs: p286
  p288 | The intermediate value theorem directly: supremum and bisection | Prove the intermediate value theorem without topology, once by taking c = sup{x in [a,b] : f(x) < L} and ruling out f(c) < L and f(c) > L, and once by bisection with nested intervals, seeing exactly where completeness enters. | needs: nothing
  p289 | Why every hypothesis of the intermediate value theorem is needed | Show the theorem fails for a discontinuous function (a step function), on a domain that is not an interval, and for x^2 - 2 on the rationals in [0,2], and see that the converse fails: a function with the intermediate value property need not be continuous (sin(1/x) with value 0 at 0). | needs: p288
  p290 | Continuous images of intervals are intervals | Prove that a continuous function maps any interval to an interval, and that it maps [a,b] onto exactly [m, M] where m and M are its minimum and maximum, combining the extreme and intermediate value theorems. | needs: p284, p287
  p291 | Proving equations have solutions with the intermediate value theorem | Use sign changes to prove that equations such as x^2 = 2, x^n = a (a > 0) and cos x = x have solutions in a given interval, and use bisection to locate a root to any desired accuracy. | needs: p288
  p292 | Every odd-degree polynomial has a real root | Prove that a real polynomial of odd degree takes both signs, because its leading term dominates for large |x|, and therefore has a real root; see why even degree gives no such guarantee (x^2 + 1). | needs: p288
  p293 | Fixed-point theorem on a closed interval | Prove that every continuous f: [a,b] -> [a,b] has a fixed point by applying the intermediate value theorem to g(x) = f(x) - x, and give counterexamples on (0,1), on [0, infinity) and for a discontinuous map. | needs: p288
  p294 | Which hypothesis does each theorem need: compactness, connectedness, completeness | Sort the theorems of this topic by what they really use (compactness for boundedness and attained extrema, connectedness for intermediate values, completeness of R behind both) and, given a new statement, predict which hypothesis it needs and find a counterexample without it. | needs: p285, p289

Piece: Uniform continuity
Topic t21: Uniform continuity
  p258 | Uniform continuity versus continuity: the order of the quantifiers | The learner can state the definition of uniform continuity on a set A and write it side by side with continuity at every point of A, seeing that the only change is moving 'for all x' after 'there exists delta', so delta may depend on epsilon but not on the point. They can explain why uniform continuity implies continuity and not the reverse. | needs: nothing
  p259 | Negating uniform continuity | The learner can write out correctly what it means for f not to be uniformly continuous on A: there is an epsilon_0 > 0 such that for every delta > 0 there are x, y in A with |x - y| < delta and |f(x) - f(y)| >= epsilon_0, and can compare it with the negation of continuity at a point. | needs: p258
  p260 | Proving uniform continuity from the definition | The learner can do the scratch work and write a proof that a given function is uniformly continuous by finding a delta depending only on epsilon, for example x^2 on [0,1] (delta = epsilon/2), 1/x on [1, infinity) (delta = epsilon) and sqrt x on [0, infinity) (using |sqrt x - sqrt y| <= sqrt|x - y|, delta = epsilon^2). | needs: p258
  p261 | Sequential criterion for failure of uniform continuity | The learner can state and prove that f fails to be uniformly continuous on A exactly when there are an epsilon_0 > 0 and sequences (x_n), (y_n) in A with x_n - y_n -> 0 but |f(x_n) - f(y_n)| >= epsilon_0 for all n, and knows this is the usual tool for disproving uniform continuity. | needs: p259
  p262 | Disproving uniform continuity: x^2 on R and 1/x on (0,1) | The learner can prove that x^2 is not uniformly continuous on R (take x_n = n + 1/n, y_n = n) and 1/x is not uniformly continuous on (0,1) (take x_n = 1/n, y_n = 1/(n+1)), and can say what goes wrong in each case: unbounded steepness at infinity, or blow-up near a missing endpoint. | needs: p261
  p263 | Lipschitz functions | The learner can define a Lipschitz function (|f(x) - f(y)| <= K|x - y|), prove that Lipschitz implies uniformly continuous with delta = epsilon/K, and show the converse fails with sqrt x on [0, infinity), which is uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz because its difference quotients near 0 are unbounded. | needs: p258, p260
  p264 | The Heine-Cantor theorem | The learner can state and prove that a continuous function on a compact set (for example a closed bounded interval [a,b]) is uniformly continuous, by contradiction: build sequences from the negation, extract convergent subsequences with Bolzano-Weierstrass, and use continuity at the common limit. They can point to the examples 1/x on (0,1) and x^2 on R to show why closedness and boundedness are needed, and know this theorem is what later makes continuous functions Riemann integrable. | needs: p261
  p265 | Uniformly continuous functions preserve Cauchy sequences | The learner can prove that if f is uniformly continuous on A and (x_n) is a Cauchy sequence in A then (f(x_n)) is Cauchy, and can show this fails for merely continuous functions with 1/x on (0,1) and x_n = 1/n. | needs: p258
  p266 | Continuous extension to the endpoints | The learner can prove that a uniformly continuous function on (a,b) has limits at a and b and so extends to a continuous function on [a,b], using that it maps Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences, and with Heine-Cantor can conclude that a continuous function on (a,b) is uniformly continuous exactly when it extends continuously to [a,b] (so sin(1/x) on (0,1) is not uniformly continuous). | needs: p264, p265
