Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Logic, sets and proof: This part covers the language and methods that every later proof uses: statements and connectives, implication, converse and contrapositive, the quantifiers 'for all' and 'there exists', nested quantifiers and why their order matters, and negating nested quantified statements mechanically (needed later to say what 'does not converge' or 'is not continuous' means). Proof methods: direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, proof by induction (including strong induction), constructing counterexamples, and proving existence and uniqueness. Sets and set operations, indexed unions and intersections, functions (image, preimage, injective, surjective, bijective, composition, inverse), and countable versus uncountable sets (countability of the rationals; Cantor's diagonal argument may be stated for decimals, with the real-number details left to the next part). The part also covers how to read and write a proof: what a proof is, separating scratch work from the final write-up, and proof-writing conventions. Edges: no properties of the real numbers beyond school arithmetic; inequalities, absolute value and supremum belong to 'The real numbers'. No epsilon definitions yet, though exercises may practise quantifiers on simple number statements. What matters for this learner: being fluent with nested quantifiers and their negation, and writing clear proofs. These two skills decide whether the rest of the course can be read and written. Keep it practical, not formal logic.
  - Statements, implication and quantifiers (your part): This part covers the logical language that analysis proofs are written in. Statements and the connectives and, or and not, read through short truth tables. Implication, what 'if P then Q' does and does not claim, including the vacuously true case. The converse, the contrapositive and their equivalence, 'if and only if', and the everyday wordings 'necessary', 'sufficient' and 'only if'. The quantifiers 'for all' and 'there exists', with domains made explicit. Nested quantifiers and why their order matters, shown with examples such as 'for every x there is a y with y > x' versus 'there is a y with y > x for every x'. Negating statements mechanically: De Morgan's laws, the negation of an implication, and pushing 'not' through a chain of nested quantifiers. Exercises use simple statements about integers and familiar numbers, and include previews of the shape 'for all ... there exists ... such that for all ...' without epsilon definitions. Edges: no formal logic (no deduction systems, no formal semantics), and no proof methods; using these forms to build proofs belongs to 'Proof methods and writing proofs'. Set notation beyond 'x is in A' belongs to 'Sets and functions'. No properties of the real numbers beyond school arithmetic, and no epsilon definitions of limits or continuity. What matters for this learner: fluency with nested quantifiers and their negation is one of the two skills that decide whether the rest of the course can be read. The learner should be able to translate a sentence into quantified form, say what its negation means in words, and negate statements with three or four quantifiers reliably, because later parts need this to say what 'does not converge' or 'is not continuous' means.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Proof methods and writing proofs: This part covers what a proof is and the standard ways to build one. Reading a proof: following each step, checking what is assumed and what is shown, and recognising the method being used. Direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction (with the irrationality of sqrt 2 or infinitely many primes as classic examples), and proof by cases. How the logical form of a statement shapes its proof: to prove 'for all x' take an arbitrary x, to prove 'there exists' produce a witness, and to use a hypothesis of each kind, so that statements with nested quantifiers can be proved step by step. Disproving a statement by a counterexample. Proving existence (constructive or not) and uniqueness. Proving 'if and only if' in two directions. Writing proofs: separating scratch work, where one works backwards to find what to choose, from the final write-up, which goes forwards; conventions such as stating what is assumed and what is to be shown, naming variables before use, using words between formulas, and ending clearly. Common errors such as assuming what is to be proved or proving only an example. Edges: the logic itself (connectives, quantifiers, negation) comes from 'Statements, implication and quantifiers' and is used here, not retaught. Induction is the next part. Proofs about sets (double inclusion) belong to 'Sets and functions'. Examples stay with integers, divisibility, parity and school algebra; inequalities, absolute value and sup belong to 'The real numbers', and no epsilon proofs are written yet. What matters for this learner: writing clear proofs is the second skill the whole course rests on. The learner should practise writing complete proofs, not just recognising methods, and should be able to turn a quantified statement into the skeleton of its proof ('Let x be arbitrary ... choose y = ... then ...'), which is exactly the shape of the epsilon proofs to come.
- Mathematical induction: This part covers proof by induction on the natural numbers: the principle of induction, the base case and inductive step, and how to write an induction proof clearly. Strong (complete) induction and when it is needed, induction starting from a value other than 1, and the well-ordering principle as an equivalent form. Recursive definitions (sequences defined by a recurrence, sums and products with sigma notation, factorials) and proving facts about them by induction. Standard examples: sum formulas, divisibility statements, Bernoulli's inequality for integer exponents stated with simple numbers, and statements about recursively defined sequences. Edges: proof methods in general, and how to write a proof, come from 'Proof methods and writing proofs'. The natural numbers are taken with their school properties; constructing them axiomatically (Peano) is left out. Inequality technique belongs to 'The real numbers', and recursively defined sequences are studied for convergence in 'Sequences', which will use induction from here (for example to show a sequence is monotone or bounded). What matters for this learner: later parts use induction constantly in small steps (bounds on recursive sequences, finite sums, n-th derivatives, Taylor polynomials), so the learner should be able to write a correct induction proof without hesitation and know when strong induction is the right form.
- Sets and functions: This part covers the set and function language analysis is written in. Sets, elements, set-builder and interval notation, subsets, equality of sets and the double-inclusion proof, the empty set. Union, intersection, difference and complement, De Morgan's laws for sets, Cartesian products. Indexed families of sets with indexed unions and intersections over arbitrary index sets, and their link to 'there exists' and 'for all' (for example a nested sequence of intervals and its intersection). Functions as rules with a domain and codomain, the graph of a function, image and preimage of a set, and how images and preimages interact with unions, intersections and complements (preimages behave well, images do not always). Injective, surjective and bijective functions, composition, and inverse functions, with proofs that a given function has these properties. Restriction of a function. Edges: logic and proof methods come from the earlier parts of 'Logic, sets and proof'. Comparing sizes of infinite sets belongs to 'Countable and uncountable sets'. Open, closed and compact sets belong to 'Topology of the real line'; preimages are introduced here so they are ready for later use, but continuity is not discussed. Relations and equivalence relations may be mentioned briefly but are not developed. No properties of the real numbers beyond school arithmetic. What matters for this learner: being able to prove set identities and function properties directly from the definitions, and to read indexed unions and intersections through their quantifiers, since later parts use these constantly (for example with open covers and nested intervals).
- Countable and uncountable sets: This part covers the size of infinite sets. Finite sets, and two sets having the same cardinality when there is a bijection between them. Countable (countably infinite or finite) sets: the natural numbers, integers and even numbers, subsets of countable sets, the product N x N, countable unions of countable sets, and the countability of the rationals with an explicit listing argument. Uncountable sets: Cantor's diagonal argument presented for infinite decimal (or binary) sequences, and Cantor's theorem that a set has no surjection onto its power set, stated or proved as a short example. Edges: bijections, injections and surjections come from 'Sets and functions'. A rigorous proof that the real numbers are uncountable, including the care needed with decimal expansions, belongs to 'The real numbers', which proves it from completeness; here the diagonal argument is shown for decimals with that detail left forward. The Schroeder-Bernstein theorem may be stated, not proved. Cardinal arithmetic and the continuum hypothesis are left out. What matters for this learner: knowing that the rationals are countable and the reals are not explains why rationals are 'small' even though they are dense, which later parts use (for example monotone functions have countably many discontinuities, and Thomae's function is integrable). The diagonal argument is also a model proof by contradiction to read and reproduce.
