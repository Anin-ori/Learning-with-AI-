Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Series of real numbers (your part): This part covers infinite series of real numbers as limits of partial sums: geometric and telescoping series, the term test, the Cauchy criterion for series, series with nonnegative terms, comparison and limit comparison tests, the Cauchy condensation test (which settles p-series without integrals), the ratio and root tests (the root test stated with limsup), the alternating series (Leibniz) test, absolute versus conditional convergence, rearrangements (absolutely convergent series can be rearranged freely; the Riemann rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series), and products of series (Cauchy product) at least stated. Decimal expansions may appear as an application. Edges: the integral test belongs to 'The Riemann integral', because it needs integrals. Power series, Taylor series and series of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequence results come from 'Sequences' and are used here, not reproved. What matters for this learner: understanding that a series is a limit, so that tests are theorems to be proved, not recipes. Choosing and justifying the right test matters more than the number of tests covered.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Logic, sets and proof: This part covers the language and methods that every later proof uses: statements and connectives, implication, converse and contrapositive, the quantifiers 'for all' and 'there exists', nested quantifiers and why their order matters, and negating nested quantified statements mechanically (needed later to say what 'does not converge' or 'is not continuous' means). Proof methods: direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, proof by induction (including strong induction), constructing counterexamples, and proving existence and uniqueness. Sets and set operations, indexed unions and intersections, functions (image, preimage, injective, surjective, bijective, composition, inverse), and countable versus uncountable sets (countability of the rationals; Cantor's diagonal argument may be stated for decimals, with the real-number details left to the next part). The part also covers how to read and write a proof: what a proof is, separating scratch work from the final write-up, and proof-writing conventions. Edges: no properties of the real numbers beyond school arithmetic; inequalities, absolute value and supremum belong to 'The real numbers'. No epsilon definitions yet, though exercises may practise quantifiers on simple number statements. What matters for this learner: being fluent with nested quantifiers and their negation, and writing clear proofs. These two skills decide whether the rest of the course can be read and written. Keep it practical, not formal logic.
- The real numbers: This part covers the real numbers as a complete ordered field: the field and order axioms, absolute value, the triangle inequality and its reverse form, and how to work with inequalities (bounding, 'it suffices to show', the 'for every epsilon > 0, a <= b + epsilon implies a <= b' trick). Upper and lower bounds, supremum and infimum, and the completeness (least upper bound) axiom, including the epsilon characterisation of the supremum. Consequences: the Archimedean property, density of the rationals and irrationals, existence of square roots (sqrt 2), the nested interval property, and uncountability of the reals. The extended reals and the symbols plus and minus infinity are introduced as notation only. The construction of the reals from the rationals is only mentioned. Edges: sequences and their limits belong to 'Sequences'. The nested interval property is proved here directly from the supremum, and 'Sequences' will relate it to monotone convergence. Open and closed sets belong to 'Topology of the real line'. What matters for this learner: completeness is the single fact that separates the reals from the rationals, and every existence theorem later (monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass, the intermediate and extreme value theorems, integrability) depends on it. Learners must be able to prove statements about sup and inf directly from the definition.
- Sequences: This part covers sequences of real numbers: the epsilon-N definition of a limit, proofs of convergence from the definition, uniqueness of limits, bounded sequences, the limit laws (sum, product, quotient) with full proofs, the order and squeeze theorems, and divergence to infinity. Also the monotone convergence theorem, subsequences, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, Cauchy sequences and the Cauchy criterion (completeness in sequence form), limit superior and limit inferior, and standard limits (n^(1/n), r^n, (1 + 1/n)^n as the source of e). Edges: only sequences of numbers. Series of numbers are the next part, although the Cauchy criterion proved here is what they use. Sequences of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequential characterisation of limits of functions and continuity is proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' using results from here. Compactness is in 'Topology of the real line', which builds on Bolzano-Weierstrass from here. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned. Give it room for writing many proofs from the definition, including how to find N in scratch work and the epsilon/2 and epsilon/3 techniques.
- Topology of the real line: This part covers the set structure of the real line: neighbourhoods, open sets and closed sets, interior, limit (accumulation) points and isolated points, closure, and unions and intersections of open and closed sets. Compact sets: sequential compactness, the open-cover definition, and the Heine-Borel theorem (compact means closed and bounded), with proofs. Connected sets, and that the connected subsets of the real line are exactly the intervals. Perfect sets and the Cantor set as a key example. A short note that these ideas generalise to metric spaces. Edges: continuous functions are not studied here. 'Limits of functions and continuity' uses compactness and connectedness to prove the extreme value, intermediate value and uniform continuity theorems. Bolzano-Weierstrass is proved in 'Sequences' and used here. Keep this part short and aimed at what later parts need. What matters for this learner: compactness is the hypothesis that turns local information into global information, and the learner should be able to say why (0,1) and the whole real line are not compact.
- Limits of functions and continuity: This part covers limits of functions on subsets of the real line: the epsilon-delta definition at limit points of the domain, one-sided limits, limits at infinity and infinite limits, the sequential criterion linking functional limits to 'Sequences', and the limit laws via that criterion. Continuity: the epsilon-delta and sequential definitions, the algebra and composition of continuous functions, continuity of polynomials and rational functions, and the classification of discontinuities with examples (Dirichlet function, Thomae's function, sin(1/x), x sin(1/x)). Main theorems: continuous images of compact sets are compact, the extreme value theorem, the intermediate value theorem (via connectedness, or via the supremum), uniform continuity and the Heine-Cantor theorem (a continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous), and Lipschitz functions. Monotone functions: their one-sided limits, the countability of their discontinuities, and the continuity of inverses of continuous strictly monotone functions (for example n-th roots). Edges: compactness and connectedness are defined in 'Topology of the real line'. Derivatives belong to 'Differentiation'. Continuity of power series and uniform limits belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. What matters for this learner: writing epsilon-delta proofs where delta depends on epsilon and the point, understanding the difference between continuity and uniform continuity at the level of the quantifiers, and knowing which hypothesis each big theorem needs.
- Differentiation: This part covers the derivative as a limit of difference quotients. Differentiability implies continuity, but not the converse (|x|, and a nowhere-differentiable example mentioned). Proofs of the sum, product, quotient and chain rules, and the derivative of an inverse function. Local extrema and Fermat's theorem, Rolle's theorem, the mean value theorem and the Cauchy mean value theorem, and their consequences: zero derivative means constant, the sign of the derivative and monotonicity, and Lipschitz bounds from a bounded derivative. Darboux's theorem (derivatives have the intermediate value property even when not continuous, with x^2 sin(1/x) as the standard example of a discontinuous derivative). L'Hopital's rule with proof, higher derivatives, and Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder, used to estimate errors. Convex functions may be touched briefly. Edges: Taylor series and the question of when a function equals its Taylor series belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The integral form of the remainder and the fundamental theorem of calculus belong to 'The Riemann integral'. Limits and continuity come from the previous part and are not redone. What matters for this learner: the mean value theorem is the main tool here, and the learner should be able to use it to prove inequalities and properties of functions. Differentiation technique as a drill is out of scope.
- The Riemann integral: This part covers partitions, upper and lower Darboux sums, refinement, upper and lower integrals, the definition of Riemann integrability, and the Riemann (epsilon) criterion for integrability. It proves that continuous functions and monotone functions on [a,b] are integrable, and gives a non-integrable example (the Dirichlet function). It covers functions with finitely many discontinuities, and Thomae's function as an integrable example with infinitely many. Properties of the integral: linearity, monotonicity, additivity over intervals, the triangle inequality, and integrability of products and compositions with continuous functions. The mean value theorem for integrals. Both parts of the fundamental theorem of calculus with full proofs, and integration by substitution and by parts as theorems with hypotheses. The integral form of the Taylor remainder. Improper integrals and the integral test for series (with p-series as the example). Riemann sums with tags as an equivalent definition, and Lebesgue's criterion (bounded and continuous almost everywhere) stated without proof as a pointer forward. Edges: interchanging limits and integrals for sequences of functions belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. Series of numbers belong to 'Series of real numbers', except the integral test, which is here. Lebesgue integration is only named. What matters for this learner: the integral is defined by a supremum and infimum, integrability is something to prove, and the fundamental theorem is a theorem that links this part to 'Differentiation'.
- Sequences and series of functions: This part covers pointwise and uniform convergence of sequences of functions, with examples where pointwise limits lose continuity (x^n on [0,1]) or where integrals and limits cannot be swapped. The Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence and the sup-norm view. Theorems on interchanging limits: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous; uniform convergence lets limits pass through integrals; and differentiating term by term needs uniform convergence of the derivatives. Series of functions and the Weierstrass M-test. Power series: the radius of convergence (Cauchy-Hadamard formula with limsup, and the ratio form), uniform convergence on smaller closed intervals, behaviour at the endpoints (Abel's theorem stated), and term-by-term differentiation and integration. Taylor series, and the gap between infinitely differentiable and analytic functions (e^(-1/x^2)). Defining exp, log, sin and cos rigorously by power series and deriving their basic properties, which closes the loop on functions used informally earlier. Mentioned as highlights: a continuous nowhere-differentiable function (Weierstrass) and the Weierstrass approximation theorem, stated. Dini's theorem is optional. Edges: series of numbers and their tests come from 'Series of real numbers'. Taylor's theorem with remainder for one function comes from 'Differentiation' and 'The Riemann integral'. Fourier series are only named in the next part. What matters for this learner: this part shows why rigour was needed all along. The learner should be able to decide whether a convergence is uniform and to justify every swap of a limit with continuity, an integral or a derivative.
- Where analysis leads: This short overview part is for orientation, not mastery. It shows how the course generalises and what comes next. Metric spaces and normed spaces (the same definitions of convergence, open sets, compactness and continuity, with the real line as one example). Analysis in several variables (derivatives as linear maps, the inverse and implicit function theorems). Lebesgue measure and integration (why the Riemann integral is not enough for limit theorems, the dominated convergence theorem named). Complex analysis (why complex-differentiable functions are always analytic, unlike real ones). Fourier series, and the construction of the real numbers by Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences. Each topic is presented as one idea from the course and what it becomes, with one motivating example, without proofs or practice drills. Edges: nothing here is required by earlier parts, and no topic from the earlier parts is retaught. Practice, if any, is limited to short explanation questions. What matters for this learner: seeing that the epsilon arguments and the compactness and completeness ideas they have learned are the toolkit of all later analysis, so they can choose a next course.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
