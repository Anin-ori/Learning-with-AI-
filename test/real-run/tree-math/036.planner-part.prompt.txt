Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- The Riemann integral: This part covers partitions, upper and lower Darboux sums, refinement, upper and lower integrals, the definition of Riemann integrability, and the Riemann (epsilon) criterion for integrability. It proves that continuous functions and monotone functions on [a,b] are integrable, and gives a non-integrable example (the Dirichlet function). It covers functions with finitely many discontinuities, and Thomae's function as an integrable example with infinitely many. Properties of the integral: linearity, monotonicity, additivity over intervals, the triangle inequality, and integrability of products and compositions with continuous functions. The mean value theorem for integrals. Both parts of the fundamental theorem of calculus with full proofs, and integration by substitution and by parts as theorems with hypotheses. The integral form of the Taylor remainder. Improper integrals and the integral test for series (with p-series as the example). Riemann sums with tags as an equivalent definition, and Lebesgue's criterion (bounded and continuous almost everywhere) stated without proof as a pointer forward. Edges: interchanging limits and integrals for sequences of functions belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. Series of numbers belong to 'Series of real numbers', except the integral test, which is here. Lebesgue integration is only named. What matters for this learner: the integral is defined by a supremum and infimum, integrability is something to prove, and the fundamental theorem is a theorem that links this part to 'Differentiation'.
  - Which functions are integrable (your part): This part uses the Riemann criterion to prove that important classes of functions are integrable on [a,b]: continuous functions (via uniform continuity, the Heine-Cantor theorem from 'Limits of functions and continuity'), monotone functions (via a uniform partition and a telescoping sum), bounded functions with finitely many discontinuities (by isolating the bad points in short intervals), and Thomae's function as an integrable function with infinitely many (countably many, dense) discontinuities, with integral 0. It contrasts Thomae's function with the Dirichlet function (defined and shown non-integrable in 'Darboux sums and the definition of integrability'). It closes by stating Lebesgue's criterion (a bounded function is Riemann integrable exactly when its set of discontinuities has measure zero), with measure zero defined by covers of small total length, without proof, as a pointer to 'Where analysis leads'. Edges: the definition and the Riemann criterion come from the previous sibling. Integrability of sums, products and compositions with continuous functions belongs to 'Properties of the integral'. Uniform continuity is proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' and only used. What matters for this learner: seeing how one criterion turns into proofs, and that each proof uses a specific hypothesis (uniform continuity, monotonicity, boundedness) to make U - L small.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Darboux sums and the definition of integrability: This part sets up the Riemann integral for bounded functions on a closed interval [a,b]: partitions and refinements, upper and lower Darboux sums, the lemma that refining a partition raises lower sums and lowers upper sums, and that every lower sum is at most every upper sum. It defines the upper and lower integrals as an infimum and a supremum, defines a function as Riemann integrable when they are equal, and proves the Riemann (epsilon) criterion: f is integrable if and only if for every epsilon > 0 there is a partition with U(f,P) - L(f,P) < epsilon. Worked examples computed from the definition (constant functions, a step function, f(x) = x or x^2 on [0,1]) and the Dirichlet function as a bounded function that is not integrable. It ends by mentioning Riemann sums with tags and the mesh definition as an equivalent definition, without developing the proof. Edges: proofs that whole classes of functions (continuous, monotone, Thomae's function) are integrable belong to 'Which functions are integrable'. Properties such as linearity and additivity belong to 'Properties of the integral'. Supremum, infimum and their epsilon characterisation come from 'The real numbers' and are used, not retaught. What matters for this learner: the integral is defined by a supremum and an infimum, so the sup/inf skills from 'The real numbers' are exercised here; integrability is something to prove; and the Riemann criterion is the working tool used by every later proof in the integral.
- Properties of the integral: This part proves the algebraic and order properties of the Riemann integral: linearity (integrability of f + g and cf, with the integral of the sum as the sum of integrals), monotonicity (f <= g implies the integral of f is at most the integral of g), additivity over intervals (f integrable on [a,b] if and only if on [a,c] and [c,b], with the integrals adding) and the convention for reversed limits, integrability of |f| and the triangle inequality for integrals, integrability of the composition of an integrable function with a continuous function, and from it integrability of f^2 and of products fg. It ends with the mean value theorem for integrals (for continuous f, and the weighted form with a nonnegative g), using the intermediate and extreme value theorems. Edges: the definition and the Riemann criterion come from 'Darboux sums and the definition of integrability'; integrability of continuous and monotone functions comes from 'Which functions are integrable'. The fundamental theorem of calculus is the next sibling. Interchanging limits and integrals for sequences of functions belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. What matters for this learner: these are theorems, not rules, and each proof is a short exercise in working with sup, inf and the Riemann criterion; the composition result is the hardest and shows why it needs continuity of the outer function.
- The fundamental theorem of calculus and its consequences: This part proves both parts of the fundamental theorem of calculus in full. First, for integrable f, the function F(x) = integral from a to x of f is (Lipschitz) continuous, and is differentiable with F'(c) = f(c) at every point c where f is continuous. Second, if F is differentiable on [a,b] with an integrable derivative F' (not necessarily continuous), then the integral of F' from a to b is F(b) - F(a), proved with the mean value theorem on each subinterval of a partition. Examples showing why the hypotheses matter (a jump in f giving a corner in F; a differentiable function whose derivative is not integrable, mentioned). Consequences proved as theorems with stated hypotheses: integration by parts, integration by substitution (change of variables), and Taylor's theorem with the integral form of the remainder, compared with the Lagrange form. Edges: the mean value theorem, the chain rule, the product rule and Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder come from 'Differentiation' and are used, not reproved. Properties of the integral such as additivity and the mean value theorem for integrals come from 'Properties of the integral'. Computational integration drills are out of scope. When a function equals its Taylor series belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. What matters for this learner: the fundamental theorem is a theorem that links this part to 'Differentiation', with precise hypotheses in each half, and the learner should be able to reproduce both proofs and say what fails when a hypothesis is dropped.
- Improper integrals and the integral test: This part extends the integral beyond bounded functions on closed bounded intervals: improper integrals over unbounded intervals (integral from a to infinity as a limit of integrals over [a,b]) and of functions unbounded near an endpoint, both defined as limits of ordinary Riemann integrals. Convergence and divergence, the standard examples 1/x^p on [1, infinity) and on (0,1], the comparison test for nonnegative integrands, and absolute versus conditional convergence of improper integrals (sin(x)/x as the example, mentioned or proved). The integral test for series with a positive decreasing function, proved by comparing the series with upper and lower sums, with the p-series as the main application and the error estimate for the tail of the series. Edges: series of numbers, the comparison tests for series and the Cauchy condensation test belong to 'Series of real numbers'; this part only adds the integral test and may note that it gives a second proof of the p-series result. Evaluating integrals uses the fundamental theorem from the previous sibling. Lebesgue integration and the gamma function are not treated. What matters for this learner: an improper integral is a limit, not a new kind of integral, so its convergence must be proved; and the integral test is a theorem about comparing sums with integrals, not a recipe.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
