Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- The real numbers: This part covers the real numbers as a complete ordered field: the field and order axioms, absolute value, the triangle inequality and its reverse form, and how to work with inequalities (bounding, 'it suffices to show', the 'for every epsilon > 0, a <= b + epsilon implies a <= b' trick). Upper and lower bounds, supremum and infimum, and the completeness (least upper bound) axiom, including the epsilon characterisation of the supremum. Consequences: the Archimedean property, density of the rationals and irrationals, existence of square roots (sqrt 2), the nested interval property, and uncountability of the reals. The extended reals and the symbols plus and minus infinity are introduced as notation only. The construction of the reals from the rationals is only mentioned. Edges: sequences and their limits belong to 'Sequences'. The nested interval property is proved here directly from the supremum, and 'Sequences' will relate it to monotone convergence. Open and closed sets belong to 'Topology of the real line'. What matters for this learner: completeness is the single fact that separates the reals from the rationals, and every existence theorem later (monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass, the intermediate and extreme value theorems, integrability) depends on it. Learners must be able to prove statements about sup and inf directly from the definition.
  - Ordered field axioms, absolute value and inequalities (your part): This part covers the real numbers as an ordered field: the field axioms and the order axioms, and how familiar algebraic facts (for example 0 * a = 0, a^2 >= 0, 1 > 0, and multiplying an inequality by a negative number reverses it) follow from them. Absolute value and its properties (|ab| = |a||b|, |a| <= c exactly when -c <= a <= c), the triangle inequality and the reverse triangle inequality |(|a| - |b|)| <= |a - b|, and distance on the line. It also covers working with inequalities as proof tools: chaining and bounding estimates, the 'it suffices to show' style of argument, Bernoulli's inequality as an example, and the lemma 'if a <= b + epsilon for every epsilon > 0 then a <= b' (and its form 'if |a - b| < epsilon for every epsilon > 0 then a = b'). It notes that the rationals are also an ordered field, so nothing here yet separates the reals from the rationals. Edges: no upper bounds, supremum or completeness; those are 'Supremum, infimum and the completeness axiom'. Proof methods and quantifier handling come from 'Logic, sets and proof' and are used, not retaught. Epsilon-N estimates for sequences belong to 'Sequences'. What matters for this learner: fluency with absolute value and the triangle inequality, and the epsilon lemma, since every later epsilon proof is a chain of such estimates. Keep the axiom-level derivations to a few representative ones rather than deriving all of school algebra.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Supremum, infimum and the completeness axiom: This part covers upper and lower bounds, sets bounded above, below and bounded, maximum and minimum, and the definitions of supremum (least upper bound) and infimum (greatest lower bound), with examples where the supremum is or is not attained (for example [0,1), {1 - 1/n}). The completeness (least upper bound) axiom, which makes the reals a complete ordered field, and its equivalent form for infimum (inf S = -sup(-S)). The epsilon characterisation of the supremum: u = sup S exactly when u is an upper bound and for every epsilon > 0 some s in S has s > u - epsilon, and the same for infimum. Practice in proving sup and inf statements directly from the definition: sup(A + B) = sup A + sup B, sup(cA) for c > 0 and c < 0, if A is a subset of B then sup A <= sup B, and sup and inf of sets of function values. The set {x in Q : x^2 < 2} as an example showing that the rationals are not complete, with the full proof of sqrt 2 left to the next part. A mention that the reals can be built from the rationals by Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences, without the construction. The extended reals, and writing sup S = +infinity when S is unbounded above, as notation only. Edges: ordered field facts, absolute value and the epsilon lemma come from 'Ordered field axioms, absolute value and inequalities'. The Archimedean property, density, square roots, nested intervals and uncountability are 'Consequences of completeness'. limsup and liminf belong to 'Sequences'. What matters for this learner: completeness is the one property that separates the reals from the rationals, and the learner must be able to prove statements about sup and inf straight from the definition, using the epsilon characterisation. Give room to worked proofs and to the scratch-work habit of proving 'upper bound' and 'least' as two separate steps.
- Consequences of completeness: This part covers the first theorems proved from the completeness axiom. The Archimedean property (the natural numbers are not bounded above; for every epsilon > 0 there is n with 1/n < epsilon), with the proof by contradiction from the supremum, and its use to show inf {1/n} = 0. Density of the rationals in the reals and density of the irrationals. Existence of a positive real x with x^2 = 2 (and, by the same method, n-th roots of positive numbers), proved from the supremum of {x : x^2 < 2}, together with the proof that sqrt 2 is irrational, so the rationals lack it. The nested interval property, proved directly from the supremum of the left endpoints, with examples showing that it fails for open intervals and for the rationals. Uncountability of the reals, proved either by nested intervals or by Cantor's diagonal argument with the care needed for decimal expansions such as 0.4999... = 0.5. Edges: the definitions of sup, inf and completeness come from 'Supremum, infimum and the completeness axiom'. The definition of countable sets and the diagonal argument for decimals in outline come from 'Logic, sets and proof'. 'Sequences' relates nested intervals to the monotone convergence theorem and proves Bolzano-Weierstrass. Open and closed sets and the Cantor set belong to 'Topology of the real line'. Continuity of n-th root functions belongs to 'Limits of functions and continuity'. What matters for this learner: these are model existence proofs (a number is produced as a supremum and then shown to have the property), the same pattern used later for monotone convergence, the intermediate value theorem and integrability; and the Archimedean property is the everyday tool for choosing N in later epsilon proofs.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
