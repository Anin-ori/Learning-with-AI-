Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Sequences (your part): This part covers sequences of real numbers: the epsilon-N definition of a limit, proofs of convergence from the definition, uniqueness of limits, bounded sequences, the limit laws (sum, product, quotient) with full proofs, the order and squeeze theorems, and divergence to infinity. Also the monotone convergence theorem, subsequences, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, Cauchy sequences and the Cauchy criterion (completeness in sequence form), limit superior and limit inferior, and standard limits (n^(1/n), r^n, (1 + 1/n)^n as the source of e). Edges: only sequences of numbers. Series of numbers are the next part, although the Cauchy criterion proved here is what they use. Sequences of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequential characterisation of limits of functions and continuity is proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' using results from here. Compactness is in 'Topology of the real line', which builds on Bolzano-Weierstrass from here. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned. Give it room for writing many proofs from the definition, including how to find N in scratch work and the epsilon/2 and epsilon/3 techniques.

Piece: Convergence from the definition
Topic t8: Convergence from the definition
  p84 | What a sequence is | The learner understands a sequence as a function from the natural numbers to the reals, reads the notations (a_n), a_n and a: N -> R, and can work with sequences given by a formula (1/n, (-1)^n, n^2) or by a recursion (a_1 = 1, a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2)/2), computing and plotting early terms. | needs: nothing
  p85 | The epsilon-N definition of convergence | The learner can state the definition a_n -> L (for every epsilon > 0 there is N such that |a_n - L| < epsilon for all n >= N), read it quantifier by quantifier, and draw it as an epsilon-band around L that the tail of the sequence enters and never leaves. | needs: p84
  p86 | What N depends on, and harmless variations of the definition | The learner understands that N may depend on epsilon (but not on n), that any larger N also works, and that replacing < epsilon by <= epsilon, or epsilon by a fixed multiple such as 2 epsilon, gives an equivalent definition, so a proof may end with |a_n - L| < C epsilon. | needs: p85
  p87 | Tails: finitely many terms do not matter | The learner can show from the definition that changing, adding or deleting finitely many terms, or shifting the index (a_{n+k} instead of a_n), does not change whether a sequence converges or what its limit is, and so can use 'eventually' arguments. | needs: p85
  p88 | Finding N in scratch work: first examples | The learner can find N for simple sequences such as 1/n, 5/n^2 and 1/sqrt(n) by writing |a_n - L| < epsilon, solving for n, and using the Archimedean property to choose a natural number N that is large enough. | needs: p85
  p89 | Writing the final proof from the scratch work | The learner can turn scratch work into a clean proof that reads in the order 'let epsilon > 0, choose N = ..., then for n >= N, |a_n - L| <= ... < epsilon', and understands why the scratch work (which runs backwards from the goal) is not itself the proof. | needs: p88
  p90 | Bounding |a_n - L| by something simpler | The learner can handle rational sequences such as (3n+1)/(2n+5) -> 3/2 and (n^2+1)/(n^2-n) -> 1 by simplifying |a_n - L| and overestimating it (shrinking the denominator, enlarging the numerator, assuming n is past some threshold), then taking N as the maximum of the several thresholds needed. | needs: p89
  p91 | Square roots and rationalising: sqrt(n+1) - sqrt(n) | The learner can prove limits involving square roots, such as sqrt(n+1) - sqrt(n) -> 0, by multiplying by the conjugate to get 1/(sqrt(n+1) + sqrt(n)) and bounding it by 1/(2 sqrt(n)), choosing N from the simpler bound. | needs: p90
  p92 | Negating the definition: a_n does not converge to L | The learner can write the negation 'there exists epsilon > 0 such that for every N there is n >= N with |a_n - L| >= epsilon' and use it to prove that a given sequence does not converge to a given number, by exhibiting one bad epsilon. | needs: p85
  p93 | Proving a sequence diverges: (-1)^n | The learner understands that a divergent sequence is one that converges to no L at all, and can prove that (-1)^n diverges by showing, for every candidate L, that epsilon = 1 (or 1/2) is a bad epsilon because consecutive terms are 2 apart. | needs: p92
  p94 | Uniqueness of limits | The learner can prove that a sequence has at most one limit, using the epsilon/2 technique (choose N for epsilon/2 for each limit, take the larger N and apply the triangle inequality), which justifies writing lim a_n = L. | needs: p86
  p95 | Convergent sequences are bounded | The learner can prove that every convergent sequence is bounded (use epsilon = 1 to bound the tail by |L| + 1, then take the maximum with the finitely many earlier terms), and use the contrapositive to show that an unbounded sequence such as n diverges. | needs: p85
  p96 | Divergence to infinity: the M-N definition | The learner can state a_n -> +infinity (for every M there is N such that a_n > M for all n >= N) and the matching definition for -infinity, and prove such statements from the definition for examples like sqrt(n), n^2 - 5n and -n^3 + n, using the same scratch-work method with M in place of epsilon. | needs: p89
  p97 | Tending to infinity versus unbounded versus divergent | The learner understands that a sequence tending to plus or minus infinity does not converge (it is divergent), that unbounded does not imply tending to infinity, as (-1)^n n shows, and can prove such a sequence does not tend to infinity by negating the M-N definition. | needs: p95, p96, p92

Piece: Limit laws, order and squeeze
Topic t11: Limit laws, order and squeeze
  p126 | Constant multiple law | The learner can prove that if a_n -> L then c*a_n -> cL by running the definition with epsilon/|c|, treat the case c = 0 separately, and see this as the simplest example of choosing the epsilon to feed into a known limit. | needs: nothing
  p127 | Sum and difference law (the epsilon/2 technique) | The learner can prove that if a_n -> a and b_n -> b then a_n + b_n -> a + b and a_n - b_n -> a - b, by applying each hypothesis with epsilon/2, taking N as the larger of the two thresholds and combining with the triangle inequality. They can reuse this splitting pattern in other proofs. | needs: nothing
  p128 | Null sequences and bounded times null | The learner knows that a_n -> L exactly when a_n - L -> 0, and that a_n -> 0 exactly when |a_n| -> 0. They can prove that a bounded sequence times a sequence tending to 0 tends to 0, and use this on examples such as sin(n)/n. | needs: nothing
  p129 | Product law | The learner can prove that a_n b_n -> ab by writing a_n b_n - ab = a_n(b_n - b) + b(a_n - a), bounding |a_n| by a constant M because convergent sequences are bounded, and choosing the two thresholds so that each piece is below epsilon/2. They understand why boundedness is needed and can reproduce and adapt the add-and-subtract step. | needs: p127
  p130 | A nonzero limit keeps the sequence away from zero | The learner can prove that if b_n -> b with b != 0, then from some N on |b_n| > |b|/2, by applying the definition with epsilon = |b|/2 and the reverse triangle inequality. They see why this fails when b = 0. | needs: nothing
  p131 | Reciprocal and quotient law | The learner can prove that 1/b_n -> 1/b when b_n -> b and b != 0 (with b_n != 0), by bounding |1/b_n - 1/b| = |b - b_n|/(|b_n||b|) using the lower bound on |b_n|. They then obtain a_n/b_n -> a/b from the product law, and know exactly which hypotheses the quotient law needs. | needs: p129, p130
  p132 | Using the algebraic limit theorem | The learner can compute limits such as (3n^2 + 1)/(2n^2 - n) by dividing by the highest power and applying the laws step by step, justifying each step. They know that the laws need the component limits to exist, and can give counterexamples to false converses (for example a_n + b_n can converge while a_n and b_n do not). | needs: p126, p127, p129, p131
  p133 | Order limit theorem | The learner can prove by contradiction that if a_n >= 0 for all large n and a_n -> a then a >= 0, and deduce that a_n <= b_n implies lim a_n <= lim b_n and that c <= a_n <= d implies c <= lim a_n <= d. They know that strict inequalities can become equalities in the limit, as 1/n > 0 with limit 0 shows. | needs: p127
  p134 | Squeeze theorem | The learner can prove that if a_n <= x_n <= b_n eventually and a_n, b_n both tend to L, then x_n -> L, directly from the definition (and see why the order limit theorem alone does not give it, since it assumes the limit exists). They can apply it to examples such as sin(n)/n and sqrt(n^2 + 1) - n. | needs: nothing
  p135 | Limit of |a_n| | The learner can prove that a_n -> a implies |a_n| -> |a| using the reverse triangle inequality, and knows that the converse fails in general (for example (-1)^n) but holds when a = 0. | needs: nothing
  p136 | Limit of sqrt(a_n) | The learner can prove that if a_n >= 0 and a_n -> a then sqrt(a_n) -> sqrt(a), handling a = 0 directly from the definition and a > 0 by rationalising: |sqrt(a_n) - sqrt(a)| = |a_n - a|/(sqrt(a_n) + sqrt(a)) <= |a_n - a|/sqrt(a). | needs: p133
  p137 | The epsilon/3 technique | The learner can bound a distance by a chain of three terms with the triangle inequality, |x - z| <= |x - y| + |y - w| + |w - z|, and make each term less than epsilon/3, working through an example such as the limit of a_n + b_n + c_n or of a_n b_n c_n. They recognise this as the general pattern of splitting epsilon into as many pieces as the estimate has, which later proofs (Cauchy criterion, uniform limits) reuse. | needs: p127
  p138 | Limit laws for sequences tending to infinity | The learner can prove that if a_n -> +infinity then a_n + b_n -> +infinity when b_n is bounded below, c*a_n -> +infinity for c > 0, a_n b_n -> +infinity when b_n -> b > 0, and 1/a_n -> 0; and conversely that 1/a_n -> +infinity when a_n -> 0 with a_n > 0. They can use comparison: a_n <= b_n and a_n -> +infinity imply b_n -> +infinity. | needs: p130
  p139 | Indeterminate forms | The learner knows why no general law exists for infinity - infinity, 0 * infinity, infinity/infinity and 0/0, and can give pairs of sequences showing that each form can lead to any finite limit, to infinity or to no limit. They can resolve such limits by algebraic rewriting before applying the laws. | needs: p132, p138
  p140 | Bernoulli's inequality and r^n for |r| < 1 | The learner can prove Bernoulli's inequality (1 + h)^n >= 1 + nh for h >= -1 by induction, and use it to show r^n -> 0 for |r| < 1 by writing 1/|r| = 1 + h and squeezing |r|^n between 0 and 1/(nh). They can also show that r^n diverges for |r| > 1 and for r = -1. | needs: p134, p138
  p141 | c^(1/n) tends to 1 | The learner can prove that c^(1/n) -> 1 for c > 1 by writing c^(1/n) = 1 + h_n and using Bernoulli's inequality to get 0 <= h_n <= (c - 1)/n, then handle 0 < c < 1 with the reciprocal law. | needs: p131, p134, p140
  p142 | n^(1/n) tends to 1 | The learner can prove that n^(1/n) -> 1 by writing n^(1/n) = 1 + h_n, keeping only the second term of the binomial expansion to get n >= n(n-1)/2 * h_n^2, so that 0 <= h_n <= sqrt(2/(n-1)), and finishing with the squeeze theorem. | needs: p134, p136
  p143 | Polynomial versus exponential growth | The learner can prove that n/2^n -> 0 and more generally n^k/a^n -> 0 for a > 1 and fixed k, by bounding a^n below with one term of the binomial expansion and squeezing (or by reducing n^k/a^n to the k-th power of n/b^n with b = a^(1/k) and using the product law). They can use this to compare growth rates in limit computations. | needs: p129, p134

Piece: Monotone sequences and completeness
Topic t9: Monotone sequences and completeness
  p98 | Monotone sequences | Know the definitions of increasing, decreasing, strictly monotone and eventually monotone sequences. Be able to check monotonicity by the sign of a_{n+1} - a_n or by comparing a_{n+1}/a_n with 1 for positive terms. | needs: nothing
  p99 | The monotone convergence theorem | State and prove from the completeness (supremum) axiom that a bounded increasing sequence converges to the supremum of its terms, and a bounded decreasing one to its infimum, using the approximation property of the supremum to find N. Recognise this as an existence theorem that does not need a candidate limit, and that it also holds for eventually monotone sequences. | needs: p98
  p100 | Unbounded monotone sequences diverge to infinity | Prove that an increasing sequence that is not bounded above diverges to plus infinity (and the decreasing analogue), so a monotone sequence converges exactly when it is bounded. | needs: p98
  p101 | Proving a recursive sequence monotone and bounded by induction | For a recursion such as a_1 = sqrt 2, a_{n+1} = sqrt(2 + a_n), prove by induction that the sequence is bounded (for example a_n < 2) and monotone (a_n < a_{n+1}), choosing the bound from scratch work and arranging the two inductions so each step uses the hypothesis. | needs: p98
  p102 | Finding the limit by passing to the limit in the recursion | Once convergence is known from the monotone convergence theorem, find the limit by letting n tend to infinity on both sides of the recursion. This uses that the shifted sequence (a_{n+1}) has the same limit, the limit laws, and the order limit theorem to choose the right root (for example L = 2 from L = sqrt(2 + L)). | needs: p99, p101
  p103 | Why the fixed-point equation is not a proof | Understand that solving L = f(L) only gives the possible limits if a limit exists. See counterexamples such as a_1 = 2, a_{n+1} = a_n^2, where L = L^2 suggests 0 or 1 but the sequence diverges to infinity, so convergence must be proved first. | needs: p102
  p104 | The Babylonian iteration for sqrt 2 | Prove that a_1 = 2, a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2/a_n)/2 is bounded below by sqrt 2 (by the AM-GM inequality) and decreasing from the second term, so it converges, and that the limit is sqrt 2. This shows the monotone convergence theorem producing a number as the limit of a computable sequence. | needs: p102
  p105 | The number e as the limit of (1 + 1/n)^n | Prove with the binomial theorem that (1 + 1/n)^n is increasing, by comparing the terms of the expansion term by term, and bounded above by 3, by comparing with sum 1/k! and a geometric sum. So the sequence converges, and its limit is defined to be e. | needs: p99
  p106 | e as the limit of the sums of 1/k! | Show that the partial sums s_n = sum_{k=0}^n 1/k! increase and are bounded, so they converge, and that their limit is the same e. For this, prove (1 + 1/n)^n <= s_n and, for fixed m, let n go to infinity in a truncated binomial expansion to get e >= s_m. | needs: p105
  p107 | Monotone convergence fails in the rationals | See that in Q the theorem is false: the Babylonian iterates (or the decimal truncations of sqrt 2) form a bounded monotone sequence of rationals with no rational limit. The theorem therefore depends on completeness and is not a consequence of the ordered field axioms alone. | needs: p104
  p108 | Monotone convergence and the nested interval property | Prove that, given the Archimedean property, the monotone convergence theorem implies the nested interval property (the left endpoints increase and are bounded) and conversely (trap a bounded increasing sequence in bisected nested intervals). Conclude that monotone convergence is an equivalent form of completeness. | needs: p99

Piece: Subsequences, Bolzano-Weierstrass and Cauchy sequences
Topic t10: Subsequences, Bolzano-Weierstrass and Cauchy sequences
  p109 | Subsequences | Define a subsequence (a_{n_k}) using a strictly increasing sequence of indices n_1 < n_2 < ..., tell subsequences apart from sequences that are not subsequences (repeated or reordered terms), and prove by induction that n_k >= k. | needs: nothing
  p110 | Subsequences of a convergent sequence | Prove from the epsilon-N definition that every subsequence of a sequence converging to L also converges to L, using n_k >= k to carry the N over from the sequence to the subsequence. | needs: p109
  p111 | Proving divergence with subsequences | Prove that a sequence diverges by finding two subsequences with different limits, or one subsequence that does not converge, for example (-1)^n or sin(n pi / 3), and explain why this works. | needs: p110
  p112 | Every sequence has a monotone subsequence | Prove the peak-point lemma: using peak terms (terms larger than every later term), every real sequence has a monotone subsequence, splitting into the cases of infinitely many and finitely many peaks. | needs: p109
  p113 | The Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem | State and prove that every bounded sequence of reals has a convergent subsequence, by combining the monotone subsequence lemma with the monotone convergence theorem, and see that the limit is not named in advance. | needs: p112
  p114 | Bolzano-Weierstrass by bisection | Prove Bolzano-Weierstrass a second way: repeatedly halve an interval containing the sequence, keep a half holding infinitely many terms, pick one term from each interval with increasing indices, and use the nested interval property to find the limit of the chosen subsequence. | needs: p109
  p115 | Subsequential limits | Define a subsequential limit (limit point of a sequence), find all of them for examples such as (-1)^n + 1/n, and prove that a bounded sequence converges exactly when all its convergent subsequences have the same limit. | needs: p110, p113
  p116 | Cauchy sequences | State the definition of a Cauchy sequence (for every epsilon > 0 there is N with |a_m - a_n| < epsilon for all m, n >= N), compare it with the definition of convergence, which needs a limit L, and prove that every Cauchy sequence is bounded. | needs: nothing
  p117 | Convergent sequences are Cauchy | Prove with the epsilon/2 technique that every convergent sequence is Cauchy, by passing through the limit: |a_m - a_n| <= |a_m - L| + |L - a_n|. | needs: p116
  p118 | The Cauchy criterion | Prove that every Cauchy sequence of reals converges: Bolzano-Weierstrass gives a convergent subsequence, and the Cauchy property pulls the whole sequence to its limit. Understand that this lets one prove convergence without knowing or guessing the limit. | needs: p113, p116, p117
  p119 | Cauchy criterion and completeness | Give a Cauchy sequence of rationals with no rational limit, such as the decimal truncations of sqrt 2. Explain that the Cauchy criterion is a form of completeness that holds in the reals but not in the rationals, alongside the supremum axiom, the nested interval property and monotone convergence. | needs: p117, p118
  p120 | Contractive sequences | Prove that a contractive sequence, with |a_{n+1} - a_n| <= c|a_n - a_{n-1}| for a fixed c < 1, is Cauchy and so converges, by bounding |a_m - a_n| with a geometric sum. Apply this to a recursion such as a_{n+1} = 1/(2 + a_n), including an error bound for the limit. | needs: p118
  p121 | Limit superior and limit inferior | For a bounded sequence, define limsup a_n and liminf a_n as the limits of the decreasing sequence sup_{k>=n} a_k and the increasing sequence inf_{k>=n} a_k, prove that both limits exist and that liminf <= limsup, and compute them for examples. | needs: nothing
  p122 | Epsilon characterisation of limsup and liminf | Prove and use the characterisation: for every epsilon > 0, a_n < limsup + epsilon for all large n, and a_n > limsup - epsilon for infinitely many n, with the matching statement for liminf. | needs: p121
  p123 | limsup and liminf as extreme subsequential limits | Prove that limsup a_n is the largest subsequential limit and liminf a_n the smallest, by building a subsequence that converges to limsup and showing that no subsequence can have a larger limit. | needs: p115, p122
  p124 | Convergence exactly when limsup equals liminf | Prove that a bounded sequence converges if and only if limsup a_n = liminf a_n, and that the common value is then the limit, by squeezing a_n between the tail infimum and the tail supremum. | needs: p121
  p125 | limsup and liminf of unbounded sequences | Extend limsup and liminf to every sequence using plus and minus infinity (limsup = +infinity exactly when the sequence is unbounded above), and check that the earlier results still hold, including that a_n tends to +infinity exactly when liminf a_n = +infinity. | needs: p121, p124
