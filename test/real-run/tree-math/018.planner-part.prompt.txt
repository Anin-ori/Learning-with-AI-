Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Sequences: This part covers sequences of real numbers: the epsilon-N definition of a limit, proofs of convergence from the definition, uniqueness of limits, bounded sequences, the limit laws (sum, product, quotient) with full proofs, the order and squeeze theorems, and divergence to infinity. Also the monotone convergence theorem, subsequences, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, Cauchy sequences and the Cauchy criterion (completeness in sequence form), limit superior and limit inferior, and standard limits (n^(1/n), r^n, (1 + 1/n)^n as the source of e). Edges: only sequences of numbers. Series of numbers are the next part, although the Cauchy criterion proved here is what they use. Sequences of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequential characterisation of limits of functions and continuity is proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' using results from here. Compactness is in 'Topology of the real line', which builds on Bolzano-Weierstrass from here. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned. Give it room for writing many proofs from the definition, including how to find N in scratch work and the epsilon/2 and epsilon/3 techniques.
  - Convergence from the definition (your part): This part covers what a sequence is (a function from the natural numbers to the reals, with notation and examples such as 1/n, (-1)^n and recursively defined sequences) and the epsilon-N definition of convergence, read quantifier by quantifier and drawn as an epsilon-band that the tail of the sequence enters and stays in. The main work is proving convergence directly from the definition: the scratch-work method of finding N (simplify |a_n - L|, bound it above by something simpler, then solve for n using the Archimedean property), worked on progressively harder examples (1/n, (3n+1)/(2n+5), 1/sqrt(n), (n^2+1)/(n^2-n), sqrt(n+1)-sqrt(n)), and separating the scratch work from the final written proof. Proving non-convergence by negating the definition (for example (-1)^n), uniqueness of limits (a first use of the epsilon/2 technique), convergent sequences are bounded, and divergence to plus or minus infinity with its own M-N definition and proofs. Edges: the limit laws, order and squeeze theorems belong to 'Limit laws, order and squeeze', which proves them from the definition using the techniques practised here. Monotone sequences, subsequences and Cauchy sequences come later in this part's siblings. Quantifier negation and proof methods come from 'Logic, sets and proof'; absolute value, the triangle inequality and the Archimedean property come from 'The real numbers' and are used, not reproved. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned. Give plenty of room to writing many proofs from the definition, to the scratch work of finding N, and to the habit of making the final proof read cleanly in the order 'let epsilon > 0, choose N, then for n >= N ...'.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Limit laws, order and squeeze: This part covers the algebraic limit theorem with full proofs: limits of constant multiples, sums (the epsilon/2 technique), products (adding and subtracting a cross term, and using boundedness of a convergent sequence), and quotients (first proving that |b_n| stays away from zero when b_n converges to a nonzero limit). Also the order limit theorem (limits preserve non-strict inequalities, and strict inequalities can become equalities, as 1/n > 0 shows), the squeeze theorem, limits of |a_n| and sqrt(a_n), and how the laws extend to sequences tending to infinity (with the indeterminate forms that do not follow). Standard limits that need only these tools: r^n for |r| < 1 (via Bernoulli's inequality), n^(1/n) tending to 1 and c^(1/n) tending to 1 (via the binomial expansion), and polynomial versus exponential growth such as n/2^n. The epsilon/3 technique may be introduced here on a suitable example. Edges: the epsilon-N definition, uniqueness and boundedness of convergent sequences come from 'Convergence from the definition'. The number e from (1 + 1/n)^n needs monotone convergence and belongs to 'Monotone sequences and completeness'. Limit laws for functions are proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' via the sequential criterion, using results from here. What matters for this learner: the proofs of the limit laws are the model for every later epsilon argument (splitting epsilon, add-and-subtract, bounding one factor), so the learner should be able to reproduce and adapt them, and know which hypothesis (for example a nonzero limit in the denominator) each law needs.
- Monotone sequences and completeness: This part covers monotone (increasing, decreasing) sequences and the monotone convergence theorem, proved from the supremum (completeness) axiom: a bounded monotone sequence converges to its supremum or infimum, and an unbounded monotone sequence diverges to infinity. Applications: recursively defined sequences (for example a_1 = sqrt 2, a_{n+1} = sqrt(2 + a_n), or the Babylonian iteration for sqrt 2), proved monotone and bounded by induction and their limits found by passing to the limit in the recursion; why finding a candidate limit by solving the fixed-point equation is not a proof of convergence. The number e as the limit of (1 + 1/n)^n, proved increasing and bounded via the binomial theorem, and optionally linked to the sum of 1/k!. The relation between monotone convergence and the nested interval property (each implies the other given the Archimedean property), showing that this theorem is a form of completeness that fails in the rationals. Edges: the supremum, completeness axiom and nested interval property are defined and proved in 'The real numbers' and used here. Limit laws and order limit theorem come from 'Limit laws, order and squeeze'. Series of nonnegative terms, which use monotone convergence on partial sums, belong to 'Series of real numbers'. Subsequences and Bolzano-Weierstrass are the next sibling. What matters for this learner: this is the first existence theorem, where a limit is shown to exist without being known in advance, and it is the learner's first use of completeness on sequences. They should be able to prove monotonicity and boundedness by induction for a given recursion.
- Subsequences, Bolzano-Weierstrass and Cauchy sequences: This part covers subsequences (definition via a strictly increasing index sequence n_k, and the proof that every subsequence of a convergent sequence converges to the same limit), and using two subsequences with different limits to prove divergence. Every sequence has a monotone subsequence (the peak-point lemma), and the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence), proved either by monotone subsequence or by bisection with nested intervals. Subsequential limits (limit points of a sequence). Cauchy sequences: definition, Cauchy sequences are bounded, convergent sequences are Cauchy (epsilon/2), and the Cauchy criterion that every Cauchy sequence of reals converges (via Bolzano-Weierstrass, with an epsilon/3 style argument), as the form of completeness that needs no candidate limit; a Cauchy sequence of rationals with no rational limit as the contrast; contractive sequences (|a_{n+1} - a_n| <= c|a_n - a_{n-1}| with c < 1) as an application. Limit superior and limit inferior: defined via the monotone sequences sup_{k>=n} a_k and inf_{k>=n} a_k, their existence for bounded sequences, their characterisation as the largest and smallest subsequential limits, the epsilon characterisation (eventually below limsup + epsilon, infinitely often above limsup - epsilon), and that a sequence converges exactly when limsup equals liminf; extended to unbounded sequences with plus or minus infinity. Edges: monotone convergence and the nested interval property come from 'Monotone sequences and completeness' and 'The real numbers'. The Cauchy criterion for series and the root test with limsup are in 'Series of real numbers', which uses the results here without reproving them. Compactness, accumulation points of sets and Heine-Borel are in 'Topology of the real line', which builds on Bolzano-Weierstrass from here; this part speaks only of limits of sequences, not limit points of sets. What matters for this learner: Bolzano-Weierstrass and the Cauchy criterion are the tools that later parts (compactness, the extreme value theorem, series, uniform convergence) rely on, so their proofs and statements must be secure. The learner should understand why the Cauchy criterion lets one prove convergence without knowing the limit, and be able to work with limsup and liminf both as limits of tail suprema and through their epsilon characterisation.
