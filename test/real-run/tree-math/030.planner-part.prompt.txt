Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Limits of functions and continuity: This part covers limits of functions on subsets of the real line: the epsilon-delta definition at limit points of the domain, one-sided limits, limits at infinity and infinite limits, the sequential criterion linking functional limits to 'Sequences', and the limit laws via that criterion. Continuity: the epsilon-delta and sequential definitions, the algebra and composition of continuous functions, continuity of polynomials and rational functions, and the classification of discontinuities with examples (Dirichlet function, Thomae's function, sin(1/x), x sin(1/x)). Main theorems: continuous images of compact sets are compact, the extreme value theorem, the intermediate value theorem (via connectedness, or via the supremum), uniform continuity and the Heine-Cantor theorem (a continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous), and Lipschitz functions. Monotone functions: their one-sided limits, the countability of their discontinuities, and the continuity of inverses of continuous strictly monotone functions (for example n-th roots). Edges: compactness and connectedness are defined in 'Topology of the real line'. Derivatives belong to 'Differentiation'. Continuity of power series and uniform limits belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. What matters for this learner: writing epsilon-delta proofs where delta depends on epsilon and the point, understanding the difference between continuity and uniform continuity at the level of the quantifiers, and knowing which hypothesis each big theorem needs.
  - Limits of functions (your part): This part covers the limit of a function f: A -> R at a limit point c of its domain A: the epsilon-delta definition (including why c must be a limit point and why the value f(c), if any, plays no role), proofs of limits from the definition with scratch work for finding delta (for example for x^2, 1/x and sqrt x, where delta depends on epsilon and the point), the sequential criterion linking functional limits to sequence limits from 'Sequences', and its use both to prove the limit laws (sum, product, quotient, order and squeeze) without redoing epsilon work and to prove that a limit does not exist (two sequences with different limits, as for sin(1/x) at 0). Also one-sided limits and their relation to the two-sided limit, limits at infinity, and infinite limits, with the negations of these definitions written out. Edges: continuity is not defined here; it is the next sibling, 'Continuity and types of discontinuity'. Limit points are defined in 'Topology of the real line', and sequence limits, the limit laws for sequences and the squeeze theorem for sequences come from 'Sequences' and are used, not reproved. One-sided limits of monotone functions belong to the sibling 'Monotone functions and continuous inverses'. What matters for this learner: writing epsilon-delta proofs from the definition, seeing how delta depends on both epsilon and the point, and using the sequential criterion as the bridge that lets everything proved for sequences be reused.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Continuity and types of discontinuity: This part covers continuity of a function at a point and on a set: the epsilon-delta definition, the sequential characterisation, the equivalent statement via the limit at limit points (and automatic continuity at isolated points), and the topological form (preimages of open sets are open, relative to the domain) at least for functions on R. It covers the algebra of continuous functions (sums, products, quotients) via the limit laws, composition of continuous functions with proof, continuity of polynomials and rational functions on their domains, and the use of familiar functions (exp, sin, sqrt) as continuous examples with their rigorous definitions deferred. It then classifies discontinuities (removable, jump, essential) and works through the standard examples: the Dirichlet function (continuous nowhere), x times the Dirichlet function (continuous at one point), Thomae's function (continuous exactly at the irrationals), sin(1/x) (essential discontinuity) and x sin(1/x) (removable). Edges: limits of functions and the sequential criterion come from the sibling 'Limits of functions' and are not redone. Global theorems on intervals and compact sets belong to 'The extreme and intermediate value theorems' and 'Uniform continuity'. Discontinuities of monotone functions belong to 'Monotone functions and continuous inverses'. Open sets come from 'Topology of the real line'. What matters for this learner: proving continuity and discontinuity at a point from the definition, writing the negation of continuity correctly, and using the pathological examples to test their understanding of the quantifiers.
- The extreme and intermediate value theorems: This part covers the global theorems for continuous functions that depend on completeness through compactness and connectedness: the continuous image of a compact set is compact (proved with sequential compactness, and optionally with open covers), the extreme value theorem as a consequence, the continuous image of a connected set is connected, and the intermediate value theorem, proved both via connectedness and directly via the supremum (or by bisection with nested intervals). Applications: existence of roots of equations, every odd-degree polynomial has a real root, fixed-point theorem for continuous f: [a,b] -> [a,b], and the image of an interval under a continuous function is an interval. For each theorem the learner sees counterexamples showing every hypothesis is needed: open or unbounded intervals, a discontinuous function, a domain in Q instead of R. Edges: compactness, connectedness, Heine-Borel and the fact that connected subsets of R are intervals are defined and proved in 'Topology of the real line'; Bolzano-Weierstrass and the supremum come from 'Sequences' and 'The real numbers'. Uniform continuity and Heine-Cantor are the next sibling 'Uniform continuity'. Continuity of inverse functions is in 'Monotone functions and continuous inverses'. What matters for this learner: knowing exactly which hypothesis each theorem needs and why, and seeing that compactness turns local information (continuity at each point) into global information (boundedness, attained extrema).
- Uniform continuity: This part covers uniform continuity: the definition, set side by side with ordinary continuity so the learner sees that the only difference is the order of the quantifiers (delta may depend on epsilon only, not on the point), and its negation written out. Proofs that specific functions are or are not uniformly continuous (x^2 on [0,1] versus on R, 1/x on (0,1) versus on [1, infinity), sqrt x on [0, infinity)), the sequential criterion for failure of uniform continuity (two sequences with x_n - y_n -> 0 but f(x_n) - f(y_n) not -> 0), the Heine-Cantor theorem (a continuous function on a compact set is uniformly continuous) with proof by Bolzano-Weierstrass, Lipschitz functions and the fact that Lipschitz implies uniformly continuous but not conversely (sqrt x), and the property that a uniformly continuous function maps Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences, with continuous extension of a uniformly continuous function on (a,b) to [a,b] as an application. Edges: Lipschitz bounds from a bounded derivative via the mean value theorem belong to 'Differentiation'. Uniform convergence of sequences of functions is a different idea and belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. Compactness and Bolzano-Weierstrass come from 'Topology of the real line' and 'Sequences'. The extreme and intermediate value theorems are the previous sibling. What matters for this learner: understanding continuity versus uniform continuity at the level of the quantifiers, and being able to prove and disprove uniform continuity; Heine-Cantor is what later makes continuous functions Riemann integrable.
- Monotone functions and continuous inverses: This part covers monotone functions on intervals: the existence of one-sided limits at every point (proved with sup and inf), so that their only possible discontinuities are jumps; the theorem that a monotone function has at most countably many discontinuities (via choosing a rational in each jump); a monotone function on an interval whose image is an interval is continuous; and a continuous injective function on an interval is strictly monotone. The main result is that the inverse of a continuous strictly monotone function on an interval is continuous and strictly monotone, with n-th roots (and, informally, log and arcsin) as the standard applications, and a note that the interval hypothesis matters. Edges: one-sided limits as a definition come from 'Limits of functions'; jump discontinuities are named in 'Continuity and types of discontinuity'; the intermediate value theorem and the fact that continuous images of intervals are intervals come from 'The extreme and intermediate value theorems'. Countability comes from 'Logic, sets and proof'. Differentiability of the inverse belongs to 'Differentiation', and the rigorous definitions of exp and log belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. Monotone functions' integrability belongs to 'The Riemann integral'. What matters for this learner: seeing completeness at work again through sup and inf arguments, and being able to justify why functions like the n-th root are continuous rather than assuming it.
