Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Series of real numbers (your part): This part covers infinite series of real numbers as limits of partial sums: geometric and telescoping series, the term test, the Cauchy criterion for series, series with nonnegative terms, comparison and limit comparison tests, the Cauchy condensation test (which settles p-series without integrals), the ratio and root tests (the root test stated with limsup), the alternating series (Leibniz) test, absolute versus conditional convergence, rearrangements (absolutely convergent series can be rearranged freely; the Riemann rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series), and products of series (Cauchy product) at least stated. Decimal expansions may appear as an application. Edges: the integral test belongs to 'The Riemann integral', because it needs integrals. Power series, Taylor series and series of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequence results come from 'Sequences' and are used here, not reproved. What matters for this learner: understanding that a series is a limit, so that tests are theorems to be proved, not recipes. Choosing and justifying the right test matters more than the number of tests covered.

Piece: Convergence of series: definition and first results
Topic t4: Convergence of series: definition and first results
  p36 | Series, partial sums and the sum of a series | The learner can define the partial sums s_n of a series, define convergence of the series as convergence of (s_n) and its sum as the limit, recognise divergence including divergence to plus or minus infinity, and explain why an infinite sum is not computed by 'adding forever' but defined by a limit. | needs: nothing
  p37 | Geometric series | The learner can derive the closed form (1 - r^(n+1))/(1 - r) for the partial sums of a geometric series and prove from it that the series converges to 1/(1 - r) exactly when |r| < 1 and diverges otherwise. | needs: p36
  p38 | Telescoping series | The learner can recognise a series whose terms are differences b_n - b_(n+1), compute its partial sums exactly, and prove convergence and find the sum, as in the sum of 1/(n(n+1)) = 1, using partial fractions where needed. | needs: p36
  p39 | Linearity of convergent series | The learner can prove from the sequence limit laws that the sum of two convergent series and a constant multiple of a convergent series converge to the expected sums, and knows that a convergent plus a divergent series diverges while two divergent series may have a convergent sum, so series cannot be split carelessly. | needs: p36
  p40 | Changing finitely many terms and tails | The learner can prove that changing, adding or deleting finitely many terms does not affect whether a series converges (only its sum), define the tail sum from index N on, and show that the tails of a convergent series tend to zero. | needs: p36
  p41 | The term test (nth-term test for divergence) | The learner can prove that if a series converges then its terms tend to zero (from a_n = s_n - s_(n-1)), use the contrapositive to prove divergence, and explain why terms tending to zero can never by itself prove convergence. | needs: p36
  p42 | The harmonic series diverges | The learner can prove that the sum of 1/n diverges to infinity by grouping terms into blocks to show s_(2^k) >= 1 + k/2, and uses it as the standard example of a series whose terms tend to zero but which diverges. | needs: p36
  p43 | Cauchy criterion for series | The learner can state and derive from the Cauchy criterion for sequences that a series converges if and only if for every epsilon > 0 there is N with |a_(m+1) + ... + a_n| < epsilon for all n > m >= N, and use it to prove convergence without knowing the sum, to recover the term test as the case of one term, and to show the harmonic series diverges because s_(2n) - s_n >= 1/2. | needs: p36

Piece: Series with nonnegative terms
Topic t6: Series with nonnegative terms
  p58 | Bounded partial sums criterion | The learner can prove that a series with nonnegative terms converges if and only if its partial sums are bounded above, because the partial sums increase and the monotone convergence theorem applies, and can explain that this is where completeness enters (for example, the partial sums of the sum of 1/n! are bounded rationals whose limit e is not rational). Otherwise the series diverges to plus infinity; there is no oscillation. | needs: nothing
  p59 | Comparison test | The learner can state and prove the comparison test: if 0 <= a_n <= b_n for all n beyond some N, then convergence of the sum of b_n gives convergence of the sum of a_n, and divergence of the sum of a_n gives divergence of the sum of b_n. They can use it in both directions, for example showing the sum of 1/sqrt(n) diverges by comparison with the harmonic series. | needs: p58
  p60 | Cauchy condensation test | The learner can state and prove that for a nonincreasing sequence of nonnegative terms, the sum of a_n converges if and only if the sum of 2^k a_(2^k) converges, by grouping terms into blocks from 2^k to 2^(k+1)-1 and bounding each block above and below, and can explain why the monotonicity hypothesis is needed. | needs: p58
  p61 | p-series by condensation | The learner can prove, without integrals, that the sum of 1/n^p converges exactly when p > 1: condensation turns it into a geometric series with ratio 2^(1-p), and for p <= 0 the term test applies. They recognise the harmonic series as the borderline case p = 1. | needs: p60
  p62 | Logarithmic benchmark series | The learner can use condensation twice to show that the sum of 1/(n log n) diverges and that the sum of 1/(n (log n)^p) converges exactly when p > 1, and understands from this that there is no slowest divergent or fastest convergent series to compare against. | needs: p60, p61
  p63 | Writing the comparison inequality | The learner can turn an expression such as (n+1)/(n^3 - n + 5) into a correct, proved inequality against a benchmark, by enlarging the numerator and shrinking the denominator (or the reverse), valid for all n beyond a stated N. They avoid the classic mistakes: bounding above by a divergent series or below by a convergent one, which proves nothing, and inequalities that hold only in the wrong direction. | needs: p59
  p64 | Limit comparison test | The learner can state and prove the limit comparison test: if a_n, b_n > 0 and a_n/b_n tends to a finite positive limit, the two series converge or diverge together (with the one-sided versions for limit 0 and limit infinity), by using the definition of the limit to get c b_n <= a_n <= C b_n eventually and then applying the comparison test. They can decide when limit comparison is easier than writing a direct inequality. | needs: p59
  p65 | Comparing with a geometric series | The learner can show convergence by proving a_n <= C r^n eventually with 0 <= r < 1, for instance when a_(n+1)/a_n <= r or a_n^(1/n) <= r for all large n, and sees this as the idea behind the ratio and root tests, which are stated and proved for absolute values in the next part. They can apply it to series such as the sum of n^2/2^n or n!/n^n. | needs: p59
  p66 | Choosing a comparison series | Given a series of nonnegative terms, the learner can find its dominant behaviour (leading powers, logarithms growing slower than any power, exponentials faster than any power), pick a benchmark (geometric, p-series or logarithmic), decide between direct comparison, limit comparison and condensation, and write the justification as a short proof rather than a recipe. | needs: p61, p63, p64
  p67 | Decimal expansions converge | The learner can prove that for any digits d_k in {0,...,9} the series of d_k/10^k converges, by comparison with the geometric series 9/10^k, to a number in [0,1], and can compute simple cases such as 0.999... = 1. | needs: p59
  p68 | Every number in [0,1] has a decimal expansion | The learner can prove that each x in [0,1] equals the sum of d_k/10^k for suitably chosen digits, by choosing each digit greedily (as the integer part of 10^k x minus 10 times the previous one) and showing the partial sums lie within 10^(-k) below x. They know that the expansion is unique except for numbers with two expansions, one ending in 0s and one ending in 9s. | needs: p67

Piece: Absolute and conditional convergence
Topic t5: Absolute and conditional convergence
  p44 | Absolute convergence implies convergence | The learner can define absolute and conditional convergence and prove that if the sum of |a_n| converges then the sum of a_n converges, using the Cauchy criterion for series and the triangle inequality. | needs: nothing
  p45 | Alternating series (Leibniz) test | The learner can state the test (terms alternate in sign, decrease in absolute value and tend to zero) and prove it, either by showing the even and odd partial sums are monotone and squeeze together or through the Cauchy criterion. | needs: nothing
  p46 | Error bound for alternating series | The learner can prove that for a Leibniz series the sum lies between consecutive partial sums, so |S - s_n| <= a_{n+1}, and use this to estimate a sum to a given accuracy. | needs: p45
  p47 | Conditional convergence: the alternating harmonic series | The learner can show that the alternating harmonic series converges but not absolutely, which proves that convergence does not imply absolute convergence, and can recognise other conditionally convergent series. | needs: p44, p45
  p48 | The ratio test | The learner can state the ratio test with limsup and liminf of |a_{n+1}/a_n|, prove absolute convergence when the limsup is below 1 by comparison with a geometric series, and prove divergence when the liminf is above 1 (or the ratio is eventually at least 1) because the terms do not tend to zero. | needs: p44
  p49 | The root test | The learner can state the root test with L = limsup |a_n|^(1/n) and prove absolute convergence when L < 1 by comparison with a geometric series, and divergence when L > 1 because |a_n| > 1 infinitely often. | needs: p44
  p50 | Comparing the ratio and root tests and the inconclusive case | The learner can show with p-series that a limit of 1 in either test decides nothing, prove the inequalities liminf |a_{n+1}/a_n| <= liminf |a_n|^(1/n) <= limsup |a_n|^(1/n) <= limsup |a_{n+1}/a_n|, conclude that the root test is at least as strong as the ratio test, and give a series where the root test works but the ratio test fails. | needs: p48, p49
  p51 | Choosing a test for a given series | The learner can look at a series, pick a suitable test (term test, comparison, ratio, root, Leibniz, absolute convergence first) for reasons they can state, and write the argument as a complete proof that checks every hypothesis. | needs: p47, p50
  p52 | Positive and negative parts of a series | The learner can split a series into its positive and negative parts and prove that it converges absolutely exactly when both parts converge, while for a conditionally convergent series both parts diverge to infinity. | needs: p47
  p53 | Rearranging an absolutely convergent series | The learner can define a rearrangement as a bijection of the indices and prove that every rearrangement of an absolutely convergent series converges to the same sum. | needs: p44
  p54 | The Riemann rearrangement theorem | The learner can explain, and sketch the proof, that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number or to diverge, by taking positive terms until the target is passed and then negative terms, and can see a concrete rearrangement of the alternating harmonic series with a different sum. | needs: p52
  p55 | The Cauchy product and Mertens' theorem | The learner can define the Cauchy product of two series, prove that the product of two absolutely convergent series converges absolutely to the product of their sums, and state Mertens' theorem that one absolutely convergent factor is enough. | needs: p44
  p56 | A Cauchy product that diverges | The learner can show that the Cauchy product of the convergent series sum (-1)^n / sqrt(n+1) with itself diverges because its terms do not tend to zero, so conditional convergence of both factors is not enough. | needs: p47, p55
  p57 | Dirichlet's and Abel's tests (optional) | The learner can use summation by parts to prove Dirichlet's test (bounded partial sums times a sequence decreasing to zero) and Abel's test, and see the Leibniz test as a special case. | needs: nothing
