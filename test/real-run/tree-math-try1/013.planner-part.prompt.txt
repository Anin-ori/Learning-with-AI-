Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Sequences: This part covers sequences of real numbers: the epsilon-N definition of a limit, proofs of convergence from the definition, uniqueness of limits, bounded sequences, the limit laws (sum, product, quotient) with full proofs, the order and squeeze theorems, and divergence to infinity. Also the monotone convergence theorem, subsequences, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, Cauchy sequences and the Cauchy criterion (completeness in sequence form), limit superior and limit inferior, and standard limits (n^(1/n), r^n, (1 + 1/n)^n as the source of e). Edges: only sequences of numbers. Series of numbers are the next part, although the Cauchy criterion proved here is what they use. Sequences of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequential characterisation of limits of functions and continuity is proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' using results from here. Compactness is in 'Topology of the real line', which builds on Bolzano-Weierstrass from here. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned. Give it room for writing many proofs from the definition, including how to find N in scratch work and the epsilon/2 and epsilon/3 techniques.
  - Limit laws and limit theorems (your part): This part covers the theorems that let the learner compute and compare limits without returning to the definition every time, each proved in full from the epsilon-N definition. Contents: the sum and constant-multiple laws (the epsilon/2 technique); the product law (adding and subtracting a middle term, using boundedness of convergent sequences); the quotient law (bounding |b_n| away from zero first); combining estimates with the epsilon/3 technique and the general habit of aiming for 'a constant times epsilon'; |a_n| -> |L| and the convergence of sqrt(a_n); the order limit theorem (limits preserve non-strict inequalities, and why strict inequalities can become equalities); the squeeze theorem; a sequence tending to zero times a bounded sequence tends to zero; rules for divergence to infinity (for example a_n -> infinity implies 1/a_n -> 0, and sums with bounded sequences); and standard limits proved with these tools: r^n for |r| < 1 and its behaviour for other r, n^(1/n) -> 1 and a^(1/n) -> 1 (via Bernoulli's inequality or the binomial theorem), and the comparison of growth rates such as n^k / r^n -> 0 and r^n / n! -> 0. Edges: the epsilon-N definition, uniqueness, boundedness of convergent sequences and divergence to infinity are set up in 'Convergence from the definition' and used here. The limit (1 + 1/n)^n and the number e need the monotone convergence theorem and belong to 'Completeness and sequences'. Limit laws for functions are proved in 'Limits of functions and continuity' using these. What matters for this learner: the proofs of the limit laws are the model for every later epsilon argument (epsilon/2, epsilon/3, bounding factors first), so the learner should be able to reproduce and adapt them, not just apply the laws.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Convergence from the definition: This part covers what it means for a sequence of real numbers to converge, and how to prove convergence and divergence directly from the definition. Contents: sequences as functions on the natural numbers, notation and examples; the epsilon-N definition of the limit, read quantifier by quantifier and pictured as 'eventually inside every epsilon-band'; proofs from the definition for typical sequences (1/n, rational expressions such as (3n+1)/(2n+5), sequences with square roots or sin n / n), with the scratch work for finding N (simplify |a_n - L|, bound it by something simpler, solve for n, then use the Archimedean property) separated from the final write-up; negating the definition to prove that a sequence does not converge to a given L, and that it diverges altogether ((-1)^n as the standard example); uniqueness of limits; convergent sequences are bounded; the fact that changing or removing finitely many terms does not affect convergence (tails); and divergence to plus or minus infinity, with its own M-N definition and proofs, and why such sequences do not 'converge'. Edges: the limit laws, the order limit theorem and the squeeze theorem belong to 'Limit laws and limit theorems'; here every proof works straight from the definition. Monotone sequences, subsequences and Cauchy sequences belong to 'Completeness and sequences'. Quantifier negation, proof methods, absolute value, the triangle inequality and the Archimedean property come from 'Logic, sets and proof' and 'The real numbers' and are used, not retaught. What matters for this learner: this is where the epsilon-N way of thinking is learned, so give it plenty of room for practice in writing proofs from the definition, with worked scratch work for N and common mistakes (N depending on n, choosing N before epsilon, forgetting that N must be a natural number).
- Completeness and sequences: This part covers the theorems where completeness of the reals turns into statements about sequences, all giving convergence or limits without knowing the limit in advance. Contents: monotone sequences and the monotone convergence theorem (proved from the supremum), with applications to recursively defined sequences (for example a_1 = sqrt 2, a_(n+1) = sqrt(2 + a_n), proved monotone and bounded by induction, with the limit found from the recursion); (1 + 1/n)^n increasing and bounded, defining e; how monotone convergence relates to the nested interval property proved in 'The real numbers'; subsequences, the fact that subsequences of a convergent sequence converge to the same limit, and using two subsequences with different limits to prove divergence; every sequence has a monotone subsequence; the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (via monotone subsequences or via nested intervals); Cauchy sequences, that convergent sequences are Cauchy, that Cauchy sequences are bounded, and the Cauchy criterion (Cauchy implies convergent, using Bolzano-Weierstrass) as the form of completeness that works without order and fails in the rationals; contractive sequences as an application; and limit superior and limit inferior, defined through the monotone sequences sup and inf of tails, their characterisation as the largest and smallest subsequential limits, and that a bounded sequence converges exactly when limsup equals liminf. Edges: the epsilon-N definition, limit laws and order theorems come from 'Convergence from the definition' and 'Limit laws and limit theorems'. Supremum, infimum, the completeness axiom and the nested interval property come from 'The real numbers'. Series use the Cauchy criterion and limsup (root test) in 'Series of real numbers'; compactness built on Bolzano-Weierstrass is in 'Topology of the real line'. What matters for this learner: these are the existence theorems of the course; the learner should see exactly where the completeness axiom enters each proof, why each fails over the rationals, and be able to choose between monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass and the Cauchy criterion when the limit is unknown.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
