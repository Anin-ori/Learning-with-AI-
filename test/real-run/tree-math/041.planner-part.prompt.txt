Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Topology of the real line: This part covers the set structure of the real line: neighbourhoods, open sets and closed sets, interior, limit (accumulation) points and isolated points, closure, and unions and intersections of open and closed sets. Compact sets: sequential compactness, the open-cover definition, and the Heine-Borel theorem (compact means closed and bounded), with proofs. Connected sets, and that the connected subsets of the real line are exactly the intervals. Perfect sets and the Cantor set as a key example. A short note that these ideas generalise to metric spaces. Edges: continuous functions are not studied here. 'Limits of functions and continuity' uses compactness and connectedness to prove the extreme value, intermediate value and uniform continuity theorems. Bolzano-Weierstrass is proved in 'Sequences' and used here. Keep this part short and aimed at what later parts need. What matters for this learner: compactness is the hypothesis that turns local information into global information, and the learner should be able to say why (0,1) and the whole real line are not compact.
  - Compact sets (your part): This part covers compactness on the real line: sequential compactness (every sequence in K has a subsequence converging to a point of K), the proof via Bolzano-Weierstrass that a set is sequentially compact exactly when it is closed and bounded, open covers and finite subcovers, the open-cover definition of compactness, and the Heine-Borel theorem with full proof that the three conditions (closed and bounded, sequentially compact, every open cover has a finite subcover) are equivalent (for example by repeated bisection and the nested interval property). It also covers the nested compact set property (a decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets has nonempty intersection) and explicit reasons why (0,1) and R are not compact, both with a sequence that has no convergent subsequence in the set and with an open cover that has no finite subcover ((1/n, 1) and (-n, n)). Edges: open sets, closed sets, limit points and closure come from 'Open and closed sets'. Bolzano-Weierstrass and subsequences come from 'Sequences' and the nested interval property from 'The real numbers'; they are used, not reproved. Continuous images of compact sets, the extreme value theorem and uniform continuity belong to 'Limits of functions and continuity'. Perfect sets and the Cantor set belong to 'Connected sets, perfect sets and the Cantor set', which uses the nested compact set property from here. What matters for this learner: compactness is the hypothesis that turns local information (something true near each point) into global information (true on the whole set) through a finite subcover, and the learner should be able to say in their own words, and prove, why (0,1) and R are not compact while [a,b] is. Keep it aimed at what continuity and integration need.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Open and closed sets: This part covers the basic set structure of the real line: epsilon-neighbourhoods, open sets defined through interior points, limit (accumulation) points and isolated points, closed sets defined as sets containing all their limit points, and the sequence characterisations (x is a limit point of A exactly when some sequence in A minus {x} converges to x; A is closed exactly when every convergent sequence in A has its limit in A). Also closure (A together with its limit points, the smallest closed set containing A, with the closure of Q being R as the density example) and interior (the largest open subset), the duality that a set is open exactly when its complement is closed, and unions and intersections: arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open, with (-1/n, 1/n) showing an infinite intersection can fail, and the matching statements for closed sets via De Morgan's laws. Standard examples: open and closed intervals, [a,b) as neither, Z, {1/n} and Q. Edges: compactness belongs to 'Compact sets' and connectedness, perfect sets and the Cantor set to 'Connected sets, perfect sets and the Cantor set'. Convergence of sequences comes from 'Sequences', density of the rationals from 'The real numbers', and indexed unions and De Morgan's laws from 'Logic, sets and proof'; they are used, not retaught. Continuous functions are not studied here. What matters for this learner: being able to prove from the definitions that a given set is or is not open or closed, and to switch fluently between the neighbourhood view and the sequence view, because later parts use both.
- Connected sets, perfect sets and the Cantor set: This part covers separated sets and connected sets (a set is disconnected when it is the union of two nonempty separated sets), examples such as Q and the union of two disjoint intervals being disconnected, and the theorem with proof that the connected subsets of the real line are exactly the intervals (using the supremum). Then perfect sets (closed with no isolated points), the theorem that a nonempty perfect set is uncountable (proved with nested compact sets), and the Cantor set as the key example: its construction by removing middle thirds, that it is closed and compact, that the removed lengths sum to 1, that it is uncountable (via ternary expansions or the perfect set theorem), perfect, and contains no interval (totally disconnected). The part ends with a short note that all the ideas of this topic (neighbourhoods, open and closed sets, compactness, connectedness) use only the distance |x - y| and so generalise to metric spaces, where closed and bounded no longer implies compact. Edges: open and closed sets, closure and isolated points come from 'Open and closed sets'; compactness and the nested compact set property come from 'Compact sets'. Supremum and uncountability come from 'The real numbers', geometric series from 'Series of real numbers'. The intermediate value theorem itself belongs to 'Limits of functions and continuity', which uses the intervals theorem from here. Metric spaces are only mentioned; they are developed in 'Where analysis leads'. What matters for this learner: that connectedness is the reason behind the intermediate value theorem, so they should understand the proof that connected means interval, and that the Cantor set is the standard counterexample that tests intuitions about size (uncountable, yet containing no interval and of total length zero). Keep it short.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
