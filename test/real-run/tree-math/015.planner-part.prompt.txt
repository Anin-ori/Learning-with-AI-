Where your part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Series of real numbers: This part covers infinite series of real numbers as limits of partial sums: geometric and telescoping series, the term test, the Cauchy criterion for series, series with nonnegative terms, comparison and limit comparison tests, the Cauchy condensation test (which settles p-series without integrals), the ratio and root tests (the root test stated with limsup), the alternating series (Leibniz) test, absolute versus conditional convergence, rearrangements (absolutely convergent series can be rearranged freely; the Riemann rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series), and products of series (Cauchy product) at least stated. Decimal expansions may appear as an application. Edges: the integral test belongs to 'The Riemann integral', because it needs integrals. Power series, Taylor series and series of functions belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The sequence results come from 'Sequences' and are used here, not reproved. What matters for this learner: understanding that a series is a limit, so that tests are theorems to be proved, not recipes. Choosing and justifying the right test matters more than the number of tests covered.
  - Convergence of series: definition and first results (your part): This part covers what an infinite series is and what it means for it to converge: the sequence of partial sums and the sum as its limit (divergence, including divergence to plus or minus infinity), geometric series with the closed form for partial sums, telescoping series (such as the sum of 1/(n(n+1))), linearity of convergent series (sums and constant multiples) proved from the sequence limit laws, the effect of changing or deleting finitely many terms and the idea of tails, the term (nth-term) test with the harmonic series as the standard example where terms go to zero but the series diverges, and the Cauchy criterion for series derived from the Cauchy criterion for sequences. Edges: limits, the limit laws and the Cauchy criterion for sequences come from 'Sequences' and are used, not reproved. Tests that rely on the sign of the terms (comparison, condensation, ratio, root) belong to 'Series with nonnegative terms' and 'Absolute and conditional convergence'. Power series belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'; the integral test belongs to 'The Riemann integral'. What matters for this learner: seeing that a series is nothing but the limit of its partial sums, so every statement about series is a statement about a sequence and every test is a theorem with a proof. The learner should be able to prove convergence or divergence of simple series directly from partial sums, and understand why the term test can only prove divergence.

Beside your part (other AIs plan these; leave their ideas to them):
- Series with nonnegative terms: This part covers series whose terms are nonnegative, where convergence is equivalent to bounded partial sums because the partial sums are monotone (via the monotone convergence theorem). It covers the comparison test and the limit comparison test with proofs, the Cauchy condensation test with proof, the p-series (convergence exactly when p > 1) settled by condensation without integrals, and other standard benchmark series to compare against (geometric, p-series, series like 1/(n log n) via condensation). Decimal expansions appear as an application: every decimal expansion converges by comparison with a geometric series, and every real number in [0,1] has one. The part should also train choosing a comparison series and writing the comparison inequality correctly. Edges: the definition of a series, the term test and the Cauchy criterion come from 'Convergence of series: definition and first results'. The ratio and root tests are in 'Absolute and conditional convergence', where they are stated for absolute values (they may be motivated here as comparison with a geometric series, but are proved there). The integral test belongs to 'The Riemann integral'. Monotone convergence comes from 'Sequences'. What matters for this learner: that every test here is a consequence of completeness via bounded monotone partial sums, and that choosing and justifying the right comparison matters more than knowing many tests.
- Absolute and conditional convergence: This part covers series whose terms change sign. Absolute convergence and the proof that it implies convergence (via the Cauchy criterion), and conditional convergence with the alternating harmonic series as the main example. The ratio test and the root test with proofs, the root test stated with limsup and the ratio test with limsup and liminf, including the inconclusive case (p-series) and the fact that the root test is at least as strong as the ratio test. The alternating series (Leibniz) test with proof and its error bound for partial sums. A guide to choosing a test for a given series. Rearrangements: absolutely convergent series can be rearranged without changing the sum (with proof), and the Riemann rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series (with at least a proof sketch). Products of series: the Cauchy product, with Mertens' theorem (or the simpler version with both series absolutely convergent) stated and ideally proved, and a counterexample showing that the Cauchy product of two conditionally convergent series can diverge. Dirichlet's and Abel's tests may be mentioned as optional extensions. Edges: partial sums, the Cauchy criterion for series and the comparison tests come from the two previous parts. limsup and liminf are defined in 'Sequences'. Radius of convergence, the Cauchy-Hadamard formula and power series belong to 'Sequences and series of functions', which reuses the root and ratio tests from here. What matters for this learner: understanding that absolute convergence is what makes a series behave like a finite sum (rearrangement, products), and being able to choose a test for a given series and justify it in a written proof.

When you write:
- A point is one idea a learner can learn in one focused sitting and then use: small enough for one lesson, big enough to stand on its own. Name it the way a learner would recognise it, briefly. No two points teach the same idea. List them in a sensible teaching order.
- what: one or two sentences on what the learner will understand or be able to do once they have learned it.
- needs: the numbers (1 for the first point you list, and so on) of points in this topic that must be learned first because this point can't be understood without them. Only direct needs: if A needs B and B needs C, A lists B, not C.
- outside: ideas outside this topic that this point can't be understood without, each described in a few words, exactly enough that another AI can find the point that teaches it. Only direct needs; an idea that only makes this point easier doesn't count. Reviewers link them.
- helps: the numbers of points in this topic that make this one easier but aren't required.
- desc: one sentence on what this topic is about and why its points belong together.
- where: real books, courses or references that teach this topic well, with the chapter or section where you know it. Add a URL only if you are sure it is right. Name only sources you are confident exist; an empty list is better than a guess.
