Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- The Riemann integral (your part): This part covers partitions, upper and lower Darboux sums, refinement, upper and lower integrals, the definition of Riemann integrability, and the Riemann (epsilon) criterion for integrability. It proves that continuous functions and monotone functions on [a,b] are integrable, and gives a non-integrable example (the Dirichlet function). It covers functions with finitely many discontinuities, and Thomae's function as an integrable example with infinitely many. Properties of the integral: linearity, monotonicity, additivity over intervals, the triangle inequality, and integrability of products and compositions with continuous functions. The mean value theorem for integrals. Both parts of the fundamental theorem of calculus with full proofs, and integration by substitution and by parts as theorems with hypotheses. The integral form of the Taylor remainder. Improper integrals and the integral test for series (with p-series as the example). Riemann sums with tags as an equivalent definition, and Lebesgue's criterion (bounded and continuous almost everywhere) stated without proof as a pointer forward. Edges: interchanging limits and integrals for sequences of functions belongs to 'Sequences and series of functions'. Series of numbers belong to 'Series of real numbers', except the integral test, which is here. Lebesgue integration is only named. What matters for this learner: the integral is defined by a supremum and infimum, integrability is something to prove, and the fundamental theorem is a theorem that links this part to 'Differentiation'.

Piece: Darboux sums and the definition of integrability
Topic t25: Darboux sums and the definition of integrability
  p304 | Partitions and refinements of [a,b] | Know what a partition of [a,b] is (a finite ordered set of points a = x_0 < x_1 < ... < x_n = b), its subintervals and their lengths, what it means for one partition to refine another, and how to form the common refinement of two partitions as their union. | needs: nothing
  p305 | Upper and lower Darboux sums | For a bounded f on [a,b] and a partition P, define m_k and M_k as the infimum and supremum of f on each subinterval, form L(f,P) and U(f,P), and see that L(f,P) <= U(f,P) for the same partition. Be able to explain why boundedness is needed for these sums to exist. | needs: p304
  p306 | Refining a partition raises lower sums and lowers upper sums | Prove that if Q refines P then L(f,P) <= L(f,Q) <= U(f,Q) <= U(f,P), by adding one point at a time and comparing the infimum and supremum over a subinterval with those over its two pieces. | needs: p305
  p307 | Every lower sum is at most every upper sum | Prove that L(f,P) <= U(f,Q) for any two partitions P and Q, by passing to their common refinement and using the refinement lemma. | needs: p306
  p308 | Upper and lower integrals | Define the upper integral as the infimum of all upper sums and the lower integral as the supremum of all lower sums, show that both exist, and prove that the lower integral is at most the upper integral. | needs: p307
  p309 | Definition of Riemann integrability | Define f as Riemann integrable on [a,b] when its upper and lower integrals are equal, with the integral as the common value. Understand that the integral is a supremum and an infimum at once, and that integrability is a property that has to be proved. | needs: p308
  p310 | Integrating constant and step functions from the definition | Compute the integral of a constant function and of a step function with one jump directly from upper and lower sums, choosing partitions that put the jump inside a short subinterval and computing the upper and lower integrals as an infimum and a supremum. | needs: p309
  p311 | Integrating x and x^2 on [0,1] from the definition | Use the uniform partition into n pieces to compute U and L for f(x) = x and f(x) = x^2 on [0,1] exactly, and show that the upper and lower integrals are both 1/2 and 1/3 by taking an infimum and a supremum over n. | needs: p309
  p312 | The Dirichlet function is not integrable | Show that the function equal to 1 on rationals and 0 on irrationals has every lower sum equal to 0 and every upper sum equal to 1 on [0,1], so its lower and upper integrals differ. This shows a bounded function need not be integrable. | needs: p309
  p313 | The Riemann criterion for integrability | State and prove both directions of the criterion: a bounded f is integrable on [a,b] if and only if for every epsilon > 0 there is a partition P with U(f,P) - L(f,P) < epsilon. Each direction uses the epsilon characterisation of the supremum and infimum, together with the refinement lemma. | needs: p306, p309
  p314 | Using the Riemann criterion in practice | Use the criterion to prove that a function is integrable without first finding the integral: estimate U(f,P) - L(f,P) as a sum of oscillation times length and choose P so it falls below epsilon. Use the sequence form (integrable exactly when some sequence of partitions has U - L tending to 0, and then the integral is the common limit of U and L), and recognise non-integrability through the negated criterion. | needs: p313
  p315 | Riemann sums with tags and the mesh definition | Know the tagged Riemann sum, which picks one point in each subinterval, and the mesh of a partition. See that every tagged sum lies between L(f,P) and U(f,P), and know, without the proof, that defining the integral as the limit of tagged sums as the mesh goes to 0 gives the same integrable functions and the same integral. | needs: p305, p309

Piece: Which functions are integrable
Topic t26: Which functions are integrable
  p316 | The proof pattern: making U - L small | The learner can write U(f,P) - L(f,P) as the sum of (M_i - m_i)(x_i - x_{i-1}), where M_i - m_i is the oscillation of f on each subinterval. They can explain the general plan for proving integrability: choose a partition that makes either the oscillations or the lengths small, so that the sum is less than epsilon. | needs: nothing
  p317 | Monotone functions are integrable | The learner can prove that every monotone function on [a,b] is integrable. The proof takes a uniform partition into n pieces, where the sum of the oscillations telescopes to |f(b) - f(a)|, so U - L = |f(b) - f(a)|(b - a)/n, which is below epsilon for large n. They can also note that this holds even when f has infinitely many jumps. | needs: p316
  p318 | Continuous functions are integrable | The learner can prove that every continuous function on [a,b] is integrable. Uniform continuity gives a delta such that f varies by less than epsilon/(b - a) on any subinterval shorter than delta, so any partition with mesh below delta has U - L < epsilon. They can also say why plain pointwise continuity would not be enough for this argument. | needs: p316
  p319 | Bounded functions with finitely many discontinuities | The learner can prove that a bounded function on [a,b] with finitely many discontinuities is integrable. Each bad point is enclosed in a short interval whose contribution to U - L is at most 2B times its length, and the continuous result is used on the closed pieces that remain. As consequences, they can show that step functions and piecewise continuous functions are integrable, and that changing an integrable function at finitely many points does not change its integrability or its integral. | needs: p316, p318
  p320 | Thomae's function is integrable with integral 0 | The learner can prove that Thomae's function (1/q at p/q in lowest terms, 0 at irrationals) is integrable on [0,1] with integral 0. Every lower sum is 0 because each subinterval contains irrationals. For an upper sum, only the finitely many points where f >= epsilon need short intervals, and f < epsilon everywhere else. They can contrast this with the Dirichlet function, which is discontinuous everywhere and not integrable, while Thomae's function is discontinuous only at the rationals. | needs: p316, p319
  p321 | Sets of measure zero | The learner can state the definition: a set has measure zero if, for every epsilon > 0, it can be covered by countably many intervals with total length less than epsilon. They can prove that finite sets and countable sets (such as the rationals) have measure zero, using lengths epsilon/2^n, and that an interval of positive length does not. They can mention the Cantor set as an uncountable set of measure zero. | needs: nothing
  p322 | Lebesgue's criterion for Riemann integrability | The learner can state, without proof, that a bounded function on [a,b] is Riemann integrable exactly when its set of discontinuities has measure zero. They can use it to explain all the earlier results at once (continuous, finitely many discontinuities, monotone because the discontinuities are countable, Thomae's function) and why the Dirichlet function fails. They can also see it as a pointer toward Lebesgue integration. | needs: p321

Piece: Properties of the integral
Topic t27: Properties of the integral
  p323 | Integral of a sum | Prove that if f and g are integrable on [a,b] then f + g is integrable and its integral is the sum of the integrals, using U(f+g,P) <= U(f,P) + U(g,P), L(f+g,P) >= L(f,P) + L(g,P) and a common refinement. | needs: nothing
  p324 | Integral of a constant multiple | Prove that cf is integrable with integral c times the integral of f, handling c >= 0 directly and c < 0 by seeing that multiplying by a negative number swaps suprema and infima, and so swaps upper and lower sums. | needs: nothing
  p325 | Monotonicity and the bounds m(b-a) <= integral <= M(b-a) | Prove that f <= g on [a,b] implies the integral of f is at most the integral of g, by comparing lower sums partition by partition, and deduce that a function with m <= f <= M has integral between m(b-a) and M(b-a). | needs: nothing
  p326 | A continuous nonnegative function with zero integral is zero | Prove that if f is continuous, f >= 0 on [a,b] and its integral is 0, then f is identically 0, by finding a small interval where f stays above half of a positive value; and see by an example that continuity is needed. | needs: p325
  p327 | Additivity over intervals | Prove that for a < c < b, f is integrable on [a,b] if and only if it is integrable on [a,c] and on [c,b], and then the integral over [a,b] is the sum of the two, by adding c to partitions and splitting Darboux sums at c. | needs: nothing
  p328 | Reversed limits and the oriented integral | Use the conventions that the integral from b to a is minus the integral from a to b and that the integral from a to a is 0, and check that additivity then holds for a, b, c in any order. | needs: p327
  p329 | Integrability of |f| and the triangle inequality for integrals | Prove that |f| is integrable when f is, using ||f(x)| - |f(y)|| <= |f(x) - f(y)| to bound the oscillation on each subinterval, and deduce that the absolute value of the integral of f is at most the integral of |f|. | needs: p324, p325
  p330 | Composition with a continuous function | Prove that if f is integrable on [a,b] with values in [m,M] and phi is continuous on [m,M], then phi composed with f is integrable, by splitting the subintervals of a partition into good ones (small oscillation of f, controlled by uniform continuity of phi) and bad ones (small total length, controlled by the bound on phi); and see why continuity of the outer function is needed. | needs: nothing
  p331 | Integrability of f^2 and of products fg | Deduce from the composition theorem that f^2 is integrable when f is, and then that fg is integrable via fg = ((f+g)^2 - (f-g)^2)/4, noting that the integral of a product is in general not the product of the integrals. | needs: p323, p324, p330
  p332 | Mean value theorem for integrals | Prove that if f is continuous on [a,b] then there is c in [a,b] with the integral of f equal to f(c)(b-a), by bounding the average value between the minimum and maximum of f and applying the intermediate value theorem. | needs: p325
  p333 | Weighted mean value theorem for integrals | Prove that if f is continuous and g is integrable and nonnegative on [a,b], then there is c in [a,b] with the integral of fg equal to f(c) times the integral of g, handling the case where the integral of g is 0 separately. | needs: p325, p331

Piece: The fundamental theorem of calculus and its consequences
Topic t30: The fundamental theorem of calculus and its consequences
  p355 | The integral as a function of its upper limit | For f integrable on [a,b], define F(x) = integral from a to x of f. Using additivity and the bound |f| <= M, the learner can prove |F(y) - F(x)| <= M|y - x|, so F is Lipschitz and therefore continuous, even when f is not continuous. | needs: nothing
  p356 | Fundamental theorem, part 1: differentiating the integral | The learner can prove that if f is integrable on [a,b] and continuous at c, then F'(c) = f(c), using one-sided derivatives at the endpoints. The proof writes (F(c+h) - F(c))/h - f(c) as (1/h) times the integral from c to c+h of (f(t) - f(c)) dt and bounds it by epsilon. | needs: p355
  p357 | When f jumps: corners in F | Using the sign function on [-1,1], whose integral function is |x| - 1, the learner can show that a jump in f gives a corner in F, where the one-sided derivatives are the one-sided limits of f. They can also explain why F can still be differentiable at a removable discontinuity of f, but then F'(c) need not equal f(c). Continuity at c is the hypothesis part 1 needs. | needs: p356
  p358 | Every continuous function has an antiderivative | The learner can deduce from part 1 that every continuous f on [a,b] has an antiderivative, namely F. Because two antiderivatives differ by a constant, any antiderivative G satisfies integral of f from a to b = G(b) - G(a). This is the evaluation formula for continuous integrands. | needs: p356
  p359 | Differentiating integrals with variable limits | The learner can combine part 1 with the chain rule to prove that if f is continuous and u, v are differentiable, then the derivative of the integral from u(x) to v(x) of f is f(v(x))v'(x) - f(u(x))u'(x). They can state the hypotheses that make this valid. | needs: p356
  p360 | Fundamental theorem, part 2: integrating a derivative | The learner can prove that if F is differentiable on [a,b] and F' is integrable, then the integral of F' from a to b is F(b) - F(a). F' does not need to be continuous. The proof applies the mean value theorem on each subinterval of any partition P, which gives L(F',P) <= F(b) - F(a) <= U(F',P), and then squeezes between the lower and upper integrals. | needs: nothing
  p361 | Why the hypotheses of part 2 matter | The learner can show with examples what each hypothesis of part 2 is doing. F(x) = x^2 sin(1/x) has a derivative that is integrable but not continuous, so part 2 applies although part 4 does not. x^2 sin(1/x^2) has an unbounded derivative, which is therefore not Riemann integrable. Volterra's function, mentioned only, has a bounded derivative that is not integrable. A step function has derivative 0 everywhere except at the jump, yet integral 0 is not equal to F(b) - F(a), which shows that differentiability at every point is required. | needs: p360
  p362 | Integration by parts as a theorem | The learner can state and prove that if u and v are differentiable on [a,b] with integrable derivatives, then the integral of u v' equals u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) minus the integral of u' v. The proof applies part 2 to uv and uses the product rule and the integrability of products. | needs: p360
  p363 | Integration by substitution (change of variables) | The learner can state and prove the following. If phi has a continuous derivative on [a,b] and f is continuous on phi([a,b]), then the integral from a to b of f(phi(t)) phi'(t) dt equals the integral from phi(a) to phi(b) of f. The proof takes an antiderivative of f and applies the chain rule. The learner can explain why phi need not be monotone and why the hypotheses are needed. | needs: p358
  p364 | Taylor's theorem with the integral remainder | The learner can prove the following by induction on n, starting from part 2 and integrating by parts at each step. If f^(n+1) exists and is integrable on the interval between a and x, then f(x) equals its Taylor polynomial of degree n at a plus (1/n!) times the integral from a to x of (x - t)^n f^(n+1)(t) dt. | needs: p360, p362
  p365 | Integral remainder versus Lagrange remainder | When f^(n+1) is continuous, the learner can derive the Lagrange form f^(n+1)(c)(x - a)^(n+1)/(n+1)! from the integral form, using the weighted mean value theorem for integrals with weight (x - t)^n >= 0. They can compare the two forms: the integral form is exact and needs only integrability, while the Lagrange form is easier for quick bounds. They can use either form to bound the error of a Taylor approximation. | needs: p364

Piece: Improper integrals and the integral test
Topic t31: Improper integrals and the integral test
  p366 | Improper integrals over an unbounded interval | Define the integral of f from a to infinity as the limit of the integral from a to b as b tends to infinity, for f integrable on every [a,b], and say precisely what convergence and divergence mean. Understand that this is a limit of ordinary Riemann integrals, not a new kind of integral, and that changing the lower endpoint a does not affect convergence. | needs: nothing
  p367 | Improper integrals of functions unbounded near an endpoint | Define the integral of f over (a,b] as the limit of the integral over [c,b] as c tends to a from the right, when f is integrable on each [c,b] but may be unbounded near a. Recognise that this is the same idea as point 1 applied to a one-sided limit at a finite endpoint. | needs: nothing
  p368 | Integrals improper at both ends or at interior points | Handle integrals such as the integral over the whole real line or over (0, infinity) by splitting at an interior point into separate improper integrals that must each converge, and prove the answer does not depend on the split point. Contrast this with the symmetric limit (Cauchy principal value), using f(x) = x on the real line as an example where the symmetric limit exists but the integral diverges. | needs: p366, p367
  p369 | The p-integrals: 1/x^p on [1, infinity) and on (0,1] | Prove that the integral of 1/x^p from 1 to infinity converges exactly when p > 1 and the integral from 0 to 1 converges exactly when p < 1, with p = 1 diverging at both ends, by computing the integrals over [1,b] and [c,1] and taking limits. These are the standard benchmarks for every later comparison. | needs: p366, p367
  p370 | Nonnegative integrands: convergent if and only if bounded | Prove that when f is nonnegative, the function b -> integral from a to b of f is increasing, so the improper integral converges exactly when these integrals are bounded, and then equals their supremum. This is the improper-integral version of the monotone convergence theorem and the basis of the comparison tests. | needs: p366
  p371 | Comparison and limit comparison tests for improper integrals | Prove that if 0 <= f <= g, convergence of the integral of g implies convergence of the integral of f and divergence of f implies divergence of g, and the limit comparison form when f/g has a positive finite limit. Use them with the p-integrals to decide convergence of integrals such as 1/(1+x^2), e^(-x^2) or 1/sqrt(x(1+x)). | needs: p370
  p372 | Cauchy criterion for improper integrals | State and prove that the integral of f from a to infinity converges if and only if for every epsilon > 0 there is M such that the integral from b to b' of f has absolute value less than epsilon whenever M <= b < b'. Note that, unlike for series, convergence of the integral does not force f(x) to tend to 0. | needs: p366
  p373 | Absolute convergence implies convergence | Define absolute convergence of an improper integral and prove that it implies convergence, either through the bound 0 <= f + |f| <= 2|f| and the comparison test or through the Cauchy criterion. Understand that the converse fails, which makes conditional convergence possible. | needs: p371
  p374 | sin(x)/x: a conditionally convergent integral | Prove that the integral of sin(x)/x from 1 (or 0) to infinity converges, by integrating by parts to reduce it to the absolutely convergent integral of cos(x)/x^2, and that the integral of |sin(x)|/x diverges, by bounding it below on each interval [k pi, (k+1) pi] by a multiple of 1/(k+1) and comparing with the harmonic series. | needs: p369, p373
  p375 | The integral test for series | For f positive and decreasing on [1, infinity), prove that the sum of f(k) converges if and only if the integral of f from 1 to infinity converges, by showing that on the partition of [1,n] into unit intervals the lower and upper Darboux sums are f(2)+...+f(n) and f(1)+...+f(n-1), which gives the inequalities linking partial sums and integrals. Know exactly where positivity and monotonicity are used. | needs: p370
  p376 | p-series by the integral test | Apply the integral test with f(x) = 1/x^p to prove that the sum of 1/n^p converges exactly when p > 1, and apply it to series such as the sum of 1/(n ln n) and 1/(n (ln n)^2). Recognise this as a second proof of the p-series result, alongside the one by comparison or Cauchy condensation. | needs: p369, p375
  p377 | Estimating the tail of a series with integrals | Prove that for f positive and decreasing the tail sum of f(k) for k > n lies between the integral of f from n+1 to infinity and the integral from n to infinity, and use this to bound the error of a partial sum, for example to find how many terms of the sum of 1/n^2 give a chosen accuracy. | needs: p375
  p378 | Sum minus integral: Euler's constant | Prove that for f positive and decreasing the sequence f(1)+...+f(n) minus the integral of f from 1 to n is decreasing and bounded below, so it converges; with f(x) = 1/x this gives Euler's constant and shows that the n-th harmonic number grows like ln n. | needs: p375
  p379 | Why the integral test needs a decreasing function | Give examples showing that without monotonicity the series and the integral can behave differently: a nonnegative continuous function made of ever narrower spikes whose integral converges although f(n) does not tend to 0, and f(x) = sin^2(pi x), whose series is 0 while its integral diverges. This shows the integral test is a theorem with a hypothesis, not a recipe. | needs: p375
