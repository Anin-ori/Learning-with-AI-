Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Logic, sets and proof (your part): This part covers the language and methods that every later proof uses: statements and connectives, implication, converse and contrapositive, the quantifiers 'for all' and 'there exists', nested quantifiers and why their order matters, and negating nested quantified statements mechanically (needed later to say what 'does not converge' or 'is not continuous' means). Proof methods: direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, proof by induction (including strong induction), constructing counterexamples, and proving existence and uniqueness. Sets and set operations, indexed unions and intersections, functions (image, preimage, injective, surjective, bijective, composition, inverse), and countable versus uncountable sets (countability of the rationals; Cantor's diagonal argument may be stated for decimals, with the real-number details left to the next part). The part also covers how to read and write a proof: what a proof is, separating scratch work from the final write-up, and proof-writing conventions. Edges: no properties of the real numbers beyond school arithmetic; inequalities, absolute value and supremum belong to 'The real numbers'. No epsilon definitions yet, though exercises may practise quantifiers on simple number statements. What matters for this learner: being fluent with nested quantifiers and their negation, and writing clear proofs. These two skills decide whether the rest of the course can be read and written. Keep it practical, not formal logic.

Piece: Statements, implication and quantifiers
Topic t12: Statements, implication and quantifiers
  p144 | Statements and the connectives and, or, not | Tell a statement (true or false) from a question or an open sentence like 'x > 3', and work out the truth of 'P and Q', 'P or Q' (inclusive or) and 'not P' with short truth tables. | needs: nothing
  p145 | Implication: what 'if P then Q' claims | Know that 'if P then Q' is false only when P is true and Q is false, so it claims nothing when P is false (vacuous truth) and says nothing about cause; recognise the many wordings ('P implies Q', 'Q whenever P', 'Q if P'). | needs: p144
  p146 | Converse and contrapositive | Form the converse and contrapositive of an implication, know that the contrapositive is equivalent to the original while the converse is not, and give examples where a true implication has a false converse. | needs: p145
  p147 | 'If and only if' and logical equivalence | Understand 'P if and only if Q' as both implications together, and what it means for two statements to be logically equivalent, checking equivalences with truth tables. | needs: p145
  p148 | Necessary, sufficient and 'only if' | Translate 'P is sufficient for Q', 'Q is necessary for P' and 'P only if Q' into 'if P then Q', and avoid the common reversal of 'only if'. | needs: p145
  p149 | Negating compound statements | Negate 'and', 'or' and 'if ... then' statements mechanically with De Morgan's laws, double negation, and the rule that 'not (if P then Q)' is 'P and not Q', and say the result in plain words. | needs: p144, p145
  p150 | The quantifiers 'for all' and 'there exists' | Read and write statements with 'for all' and 'there exists' (and their symbols), always naming the domain, and see how the truth of a quantified statement can change when the domain changes (for example integers versus natural numbers). | needs: p144
  p151 | Translating mathematical sentences into quantified form | Turn everyday mathematical sentences into quantified form: spot hidden quantifiers ('if n is even then n^2 is even' means for all n), read 'every', 'any', 'some', and write restricted quantifiers such as 'for all x > 0' as 'for all x, if x > 0 then ...' and 'there is x > 0 with ...' as 'there is x with x > 0 and ...'. | needs: p145, p150
  p152 | Negating a single quantifier | Negate quantified statements: 'not for all' becomes 'there exists ... not', and 'not there exists' becomes 'for all ... not', including restricted quantifiers, and see that a counterexample is exactly a witness for the negation of a 'for all' statement. | needs: p149, p151
  p153 | Nested quantifiers and why their order matters | Read statements with two or more quantifiers, understand that in 'for all x there exists y' the choice of y may depend on x, and explain why 'for every x there is y with y > x' is true for integers while 'there is y with y > x for every x' is false. | needs: p150
  p154 | Negating nested quantified statements | Push 'not' through a chain of three or four quantifiers step by step, flipping each quantifier and negating the final condition (including any implication inside it), then state the negation in clear words and check it against an example. | needs: p152, p153
  p155 | Reading 'for all ... there exists ... such that for all ...' statements | Read and negate statements of the shape used later for limits, such as 'for every k there is N such that for all n >= N, n^2 > k' or 'for every k there is n with a_n > k', and say in words what the statement and its negation mean (eventually above every bound, not bounded, and so on), without any epsilon definitions. | needs: p154

Piece: Proof methods and writing proofs
Topic t14: Proof methods and writing proofs
  p168 | What a proof is and how to read one | Understand a proof as a chain of justified steps from stated assumptions and definitions to the claim, and be able to read a short proof line by line, saying at each step what is assumed, what is being shown, and which definition or earlier fact justifies it. | needs: nothing
  p169 | Direct proof | Prove 'if P then Q' by assuming P and deducing Q, unpacking definitions such as even, odd and 'a divides b' (for example: the sum of two odd integers is even; if a divides b and b divides c then a divides c). | needs: p168
  p170 | Proof-writing conventions | Write a proof that others can follow: say what is assumed and what is to be shown, introduce and name every variable before using it, put words between formulas, justify each step, and end with a clear conclusion. | needs: p169
  p171 | Proving 'for all' statements: take an arbitrary element | Prove a statement 'for all x in S, P(x)' by letting x be an arbitrary element of S, using only what is known about every element of S, and showing P(x); understand why checking examples is not enough. | needs: p169
  p172 | Proving 'there exists' statements: produce a witness | Prove 'there exists x with P(x)' by naming a specific x and checking that it satisfies P(x), and see that the witness is chosen, not assumed. | needs: p168
  p173 | Using hypotheses that contain quantifiers | Use a hypothesis 'for all x, P(x)' by applying it to any particular object you need, and use a hypothesis 'there exists x with P(x)' by giving that object a new name you do not get to choose, keeping track of which objects are fixed and which are arbitrary. | needs: p171, p172
  p174 | Proving statements with nested quantifiers | Turn a statement such as 'for every x there exists y such that for every z ...' into the skeleton of its proof ('Let x be arbitrary. Choose y = ... Let z be arbitrary. Then ...'), with the choice of y allowed to depend on x but not on z; this is exactly the shape of the epsilon proofs to come. | needs: p173
  p175 | Scratch work versus the final write-up | Work backwards in scratch work from what is wanted to discover what to choose or assume, then write the final proof forwards from the hypotheses, so the write-up never starts from the conclusion. | needs: p170, p174
  p176 | Disproving a statement with a counterexample | Show a universal statement is false by exhibiting one object for which it fails, check that the counterexample really satisfies the hypotheses and violates the conclusion, and see that this is proving the negated, existential statement. | needs: p172
  p177 | Proof by contrapositive | Prove 'if P then Q' by proving 'if not Q then not P', and recognise when this is easier (for example: if n squared is even then n is even). | needs: p169
  p178 | Proof by contradiction | Prove a statement by assuming its negation and deriving something impossible, as in the irrationality of the square root of 2 and the infinitude of the primes, and state the negated assumption correctly at the start. | needs: p169
  p179 | Proof by cases | Split a proof into cases that together cover every possibility (such as n even or n odd, or the possible remainders mod 3), prove the claim in each case, and use 'without loss of generality' only when the cases are truly symmetric. | needs: p169
  p180 | Proving 'if and only if' statements | Prove 'P if and only if Q' by proving both directions separately, labelled clearly, and recognise when a chain of reversible equivalences can be used instead and when it cannot. | needs: p169
  p181 | Non-constructive existence and uniqueness proofs | Prove that something exists without exhibiting it (for example by contradiction or by cases), and prove uniqueness by assuming two objects with the property and showing they are equal. | needs: p172, p178
  p182 | Common errors in proofs | Recognise and avoid the usual mistakes: assuming what is to be proved, proving only an example of a universal claim, proving the converse instead of the statement, reusing a variable that is already fixed, and letting a chosen value depend on something chosen later. | needs: p171, p175

Piece: Mathematical induction
Topic t13: Mathematical induction
  p156 | The principle of mathematical induction | Understand the principle: if P(1) holds and P(k) implies P(k+1) for every k, then P(n) holds for all natural numbers n. Be able to explain why the base case and the inductive step together reach every n, and why neither can be dropped. | needs: nothing
  p157 | Writing an induction proof | Be able to write an induction proof in a clear standard layout: state P(n) explicitly, prove the base case, state the inductive hypothesis for an arbitrary k, and show exactly where it is used to reach P(k+1). Separate the scratch work of finding how P(k+1) follows from the final forward write-up. | needs: p156
  p158 | Sigma notation, products and factorials as recursive definitions | Read and use sigma and product notation and factorials, understood as recursive definitions (the sum to n+1 is the sum to n plus the next term; (n+1)! = (n+1) n!). Be able to split off the last term, shift an index and rewrite sums, which is the step most induction proofs about sums rely on. | needs: p156
  p159 | Proving sum formulas by induction | Prove closed formulas for finite sums by induction, such as 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2, the sum of squares and the geometric sum, by splitting off the last term and using the inductive hypothesis. Understand that induction verifies a formula but does not by itself discover it. | needs: p157, p158
  p160 | Proving divisibility statements by induction | Prove statements such as '3 divides n^3 - n' or '6 divides 7^n - 1' for all n by induction, rewriting the (k+1) case so that the inductive hypothesis appears as a visible piece. | needs: p157
  p161 | Induction from any starting value | Prove statements that hold only for n at least some n0, such as 2^n > n^2 for n >= 5, by starting the base case at n0 and taking k >= n0 in the inductive step. Know how to check where a statement first becomes true and why the step may need k >= n0. | needs: p157
  p162 | Proving inequalities by induction: Bernoulli's inequality | Prove inequalities by induction, where the inductive step uses the hypothesis plus one extra estimate, with Bernoulli's inequality (1 + x)^n >= 1 + nx for x >= -1 and integer n >= 1 as the key example. Know that this inequality is used later to bound powers and prove limits. | needs: p157
  p163 | Sequences defined by a recurrence | Understand sequences defined recursively, by one or more starting values and a rule giving a(n+1) from earlier terms (for example a(n+1) = sqrt(2 + a(n)) or the Fibonacci numbers), and why induction guarantees such a definition determines every term. Be able to compute terms and guess patterns from them. | needs: p158
  p164 | Proving properties of recursive sequences by induction | Prove by induction that a recursively defined sequence is bounded, positive, increasing or decreasing, or satisfies a closed formula, choosing the right statement P(n) (sometimes a stronger one than the claim) so that the inductive step goes through. These are exactly the facts later used to show such sequences converge. | needs: p157, p163
  p165 | Strong induction and when to use it | Use strong (complete) induction, where the inductive step assumes P(1), ..., P(k) rather than only P(k), and recognise when it is needed: when P(k+1) depends on cases further back, as in two-term recurrences like Fibonacci or the proof that every integer n >= 2 is a product of primes. Know how many base cases a given step requires. | needs: p157
  p166 | The well-ordering principle | Understand that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a least element, that this is equivalent to induction, and use it to write proofs by minimal counterexample (assume the statement fails, take the smallest n where it fails, and derive a contradiction). | needs: p156
  p167 | Common mistakes in induction proofs | Spot and avoid the typical errors: a missing or wrong base case, assuming P(n) for all n instead of for one k, an inductive step that silently fails for small k (as in the 'all horses are the same colour' argument), and too few base cases for strong induction. | needs: p157

Piece: Sets and functions
Topic t15: Sets and functions
  p183 | Sets, elements and set notation | Read and write sets using roster notation, set-builder notation {x in A : P(x)} and interval notation such as [a,b), (a,infinity), and recognise the empty set and the standard sets N, Z, Q, R. Tell an element (x in A) apart from a subset. | needs: nothing
  p184 | Subsets, set equality and double inclusion | State A is a subset of B as 'for every x, if x in A then x in B', prove a subset claim by taking an arbitrary element, and prove two sets are equal by showing each is a subset of the other. Know that the empty set is a subset of every set (vacuous truth). | needs: p183
  p185 | Union, intersection, difference and complement | Define union, intersection, difference A \ B and complement relative to a universal set through 'or', 'and' and 'not', and compute them for simple sets and intervals. Recognise disjoint sets. | needs: p183
  p186 | Proving set identities and De Morgan's laws for sets | Prove set identities such as distributive laws and De Morgan's laws for sets by double inclusion, translating each membership statement into logic. Disprove a false identity with a concrete counterexample. | needs: p184, p185
  p187 | Cartesian products | Understand ordered pairs and the product A x B, picture subsets of R x R, and prove simple facts about products such as A x (B union C) = (A x B) union (A x C). | needs: p186
  p188 | Indexed families, indexed unions and intersections | Read an indexed family {A_i : i in I} over any index set and the definitions: x is in the union exactly when there exists i with x in A_i, and x is in the intersection exactly when x in A_i for all i. Translate between the set notation and the quantified statement in both directions. | needs: p185
  p189 | Working with indexed unions and intersections | Compute and prove facts such as: the union of [1/n, 1] over n is (0,1], the intersection of (0,1/n) is empty, the intersection of [0,1/n] is {0}, and nested intervals behave this way. Prove De Morgan's laws for indexed families and handle the case of an empty intersection. | needs: p184, p186, p188
  p190 | Functions: domain, codomain, graph and restriction | Understand a function f: A -> B as a rule assigning to each element of A exactly one element of B, its graph as a subset of A x B, when two functions are equal, and the restriction of f to a subset of its domain. Know that range and codomain differ, and that a relation is any subset of a product (mentioned only). | needs: p187
  p191 | Image and preimage of a set | Define the image f(C) and the preimage f^{-1}(D) of sets and compute them for functions such as x^2 and sin on intervals. Know that the preimage is defined for every function, with or without an inverse. | needs: p184, p190
  p192 | Images and preimages with unions, intersections and complements | Prove that preimages preserve unions, intersections (including indexed ones) and complements, that images preserve unions, and that f(C intersect D) is only a subset of f(C) intersect f(D), with a counterexample showing equality can fail. Prove C is a subset of f^{-1}(f(C)) and f(f^{-1}(D)) is a subset of D. | needs: p186, p188, p191
  p193 | Injective, surjective and bijective functions | State the definitions with quantifiers, prove a given function is injective (assume f(x)=f(y), show x=y) or surjective (given y, find x), and disprove each with a counterexample. See how the same rule can change these properties when the domain or codomain changes. | needs: p190
  p194 | Composition of functions | Define g o f when the codomain of f fits the domain of g, know composition is associative but not commutative, and prove that compositions of injections are injective and of surjections are surjective, together with the partial converses (if g o f is injective then f is). | needs: p193
  p195 | Inverse functions | Prove that f has an inverse function exactly when it is bijective, characterise the inverse by g o f = id and f o g = id, and get bijections by restricting the domain (square root, arcsin). Keep the inverse function apart from the preimage notation f^{-1}(D), which exists for every function. | needs: p191, p194

Piece: Countable and uncountable sets
Topic t16: Countable and uncountable sets
  p196 | Same size: finite sets and bijections | The learner can define 'A has n elements' as a bijection with {1, ..., n} and 'A and B have the same cardinality' as the existence of a bijection between them, and can prove that having the same cardinality is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. | needs: nothing
  p197 | Countable sets: N, Z and the even numbers | The learner knows that 'countable' means finite or in bijection with N, can read a bijection with N as a list a1, a2, a3, ... with no repeats, and can write explicit bijections showing that the even numbers and the integers are countably infinite, so an infinite set can have the same size as a proper subset of itself. | needs: p196
  p198 | Subsets of countable sets, injections and surjections | The learner can prove that every subset of a countable set is countable, by listing its elements in order, and can use the consequences: a set is countable if it injects into N, or if N maps onto it (or it is empty). | needs: p197
  p199 | N x N is countable | The learner can show that the set of pairs of natural numbers is countable, both by the diagonal listing of the grid of pairs and by an explicit injection such as (m, n) -> 2^m 3^n, and can deduce that a finite product of countable sets is countable. | needs: p197, p198
  p200 | Countable unions of countable sets | The learner can prove that a union of countably many countable sets is countable by arranging the elements in a grid and mapping N x N onto the union, and can use the special cases that a union of two, or finitely many, countable sets is countable. | needs: p198, p199
  p201 | The rationals are countable | The learner can give an explicit listing of the rationals (fractions p/q arranged in a grid, traversed diagonally and skipping repeats, then interleaving signs) and an injection-based proof, and understands that Q is countable even though there is a rational between any two numbers. | needs: p198, p199
  p202 | Cantor's diagonal argument for binary sequences | The learner can prove by contradiction that the set of infinite 0-1 sequences is uncountable: given any list of sequences, flipping the n-th digit of the n-th sequence produces a sequence missing from the list. The learner can reproduce this as a model proof. | needs: p197
  p203 | Infinite decimals and why the reals should be uncountable | The learner can run the diagonal argument on infinite decimal expansions of numbers in (0, 1), choosing new digits that avoid 0 and 9 so that the double expansions such as 0.1999... = 0.2000... cause no trouble, and knows the rigorous proof that R is uncountable comes later from completeness. | needs: p202
  p204 | Countable versus uncountable: the irrationals and 'small' sets | The learner can deduce that the irrationals are uncountable because R would otherwise be a union of two countable sets, and understands that a countable set, however dense, is 'small' compared with an interval, an idea later used for discontinuities of monotone functions and for Thomae's function. | needs: p200, p201, p203
  p205 | Comparing sizes with injections: Schroeder-Bernstein | The learner can read 'A is no bigger than B' as the existence of an injection from A into B, knows the Schroeder-Bernstein theorem (stated, not proved) that injections both ways give a bijection, and can use it to show, for example, that [0, 1] and (0, 1) have the same cardinality. | needs: p196, p198
  p206 | Cantor's theorem: no surjection onto the power set | The learner knows the power set P(A) is the set of all subsets of A and can prove that no function from A onto P(A) exists by considering the set of elements not in their own image, so every set is strictly smaller than its power set and there is no largest infinite size. | needs: p205
