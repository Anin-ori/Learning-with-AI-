Where this part sits:
Subject: Mathematical analysis. Scope: The map covers single-variable real analysis as taught in a standard first university course (the organisation of textbooks such as Abbott, Bartle and Sherbert, Spivak and the analysis part of Rudin): the real numbers and completeness, sequences, series of numbers, the topology of the real line, limits of functions and continuity, differentiation, the Riemann integral, and sequences and series of functions including power series. The learner's goal names limits, continuity, differentiation, integration and series, and asks them to read and write proofs, so the map starts with a foundations part on logic, quantifiers, proof techniques, sets and functions. No starting level was given, so this part is included in full but kept practical: it covers only the logic and set language the later proofs use. Formal logic and axiomatic set theory are left out. The real numbers are treated axiomatically as a complete ordered field. Building them from the rationals (Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is only mentioned, because most first courses do it this way and the construction is not needed to use completeness. The integral is the Riemann integral, set up with Darboux upper and lower sums. That is the most common choice; the equivalent tagged-sum definition is mentioned, not developed. The topology of the real line (open, closed, compact and connected sets) gets its own short part, as in Abbott and Rudin, because compactness and connectedness are the real reasons behind the extreme value and intermediate value theorems. Some books, such as Spivak, fold this into continuity instead. Exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions may be used as examples from the start with their familiar properties; they are defined rigorously in the part on series of functions. Left out: multivariable analysis, metric spaces beyond a mention, Lebesgue measure and integration, complex analysis, Fourier analysis and differential equations. These are named in a short final part so the learner can see where the course leads, but they are not taught. Computational calculus technique (long integration drills, many differentiation rules) is also left out, except where a rule needs a proof.
- Differentiation (your part): This part covers the derivative as a limit of difference quotients. Differentiability implies continuity, but not the converse (|x|, and a nowhere-differentiable example mentioned). Proofs of the sum, product, quotient and chain rules, and the derivative of an inverse function. Local extrema and Fermat's theorem, Rolle's theorem, the mean value theorem and the Cauchy mean value theorem, and their consequences: zero derivative means constant, the sign of the derivative and monotonicity, and Lipschitz bounds from a bounded derivative. Darboux's theorem (derivatives have the intermediate value property even when not continuous, with x^2 sin(1/x) as the standard example of a discontinuous derivative). L'Hopital's rule with proof, higher derivatives, and Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder, used to estimate errors. Convex functions may be touched briefly. Edges: Taylor series and the question of when a function equals its Taylor series belong to 'Sequences and series of functions'. The integral form of the remainder and the fundamental theorem of calculus belong to 'The Riemann integral'. Limits and continuity come from the previous part and are not redone. What matters for this learner: the mean value theorem is the main tool here, and the learner should be able to use it to prove inequalities and properties of functions. Differentiation technique as a drill is out of scope.

Piece: The derivative and its rules
Topic t19: The derivative and its rules
  p232 | The derivative at a point | The learner can state the definition of f'(c) as the limit of (f(x) - f(c))/(x - c) as x tends to c, and the equivalent form with (f(c + h) - f(c))/h, explain why the two forms agree, and read the derivative as the slope of the tangent line and the best linear approximation f(c) + f'(c)(x - c). | needs: nothing
  p233 | Computing derivatives from the definition | The learner can prove from the definition, using epsilon-delta arguments or the limit laws, that constants, x, x^2, 1/x and sqrt(x) have their expected derivatives, writing each proof out in full. | needs: p232
  p234 | One-sided derivatives and the derivative on an interval | The learner can define left and right derivatives, knows that f'(c) exists exactly when both exist and agree, and understands the derivative as a function on an interval, using one-sided derivatives at closed endpoints. | needs: p232
  p235 | Differentiability implies continuity | The learner can prove that if f is differentiable at c then f is continuous at c, by writing f(x) - f(c) as the difference quotient times (x - c) and taking limits. | needs: p232
  p236 | Continuous but not differentiable | The learner can show that the converse of 'differentiable implies continuous' fails: |x| at 0 (one-sided derivatives differ), x^(1/3) at 0 (difference quotient tends to infinity, a vertical tangent) and x sin(1/x) at 0 (continuous, but the difference quotient sin(1/x) has no limit), and knows that Weierstrass built a continuous function differentiable nowhere. | needs: p234, p235
  p237 | Sum and constant multiple rules | The learner can prove that (f + g)' = f' + g' and (kf)' = kf' at a point where f and g are differentiable, so differentiation is linear. | needs: p232
  p238 | The product rule | The learner can prove (fg)'(c) = f'(c)g(c) + f(c)g'(c) by adding and subtracting f(c)g(x) in the difference quotient, and can point to where continuity of g at c is used. | needs: p235
  p239 | The power rule by induction | The learner can prove (x^n)' = n x^(n-1) for every positive integer n by induction using the product rule, and hence differentiate any polynomial. | needs: p238
  p240 | The reciprocal and quotient rules | The learner can prove (1/g)'(c) = -g'(c)/g(c)^2 when g(c) is nonzero, explaining why g stays nonzero near c, then derive the quotient rule from it and the product rule, and extend the power rule to negative integer exponents. | needs: p238
  p241 | The chain rule and the gap in the naive proof | The learner can state the chain rule (f o g)'(c) = f'(g(c)) g'(c) and explain exactly why the tempting proof that multiplies and divides by g(x) - g(c) fails: g(x) can equal g(c) for x arbitrarily close to c, as for a constant g or g(x) = x^2 sin(1/x). | needs: p232
  p242 | Caratheodory's form of differentiability | The learner can prove that f is differentiable at c exactly when there is a function phi, continuous at c, with f(x) - f(c) = phi(x)(x - c) for all x, and then phi(c) = f'(c); they can use this form to reprove that differentiability implies continuity. | needs: p232
  p243 | A correct proof of the chain rule | The learner can write a complete proof of the chain rule using Caratheodory's form, composing the two factorisations so that no division by g(x) - g(c) is ever needed. | needs: p241, p242
  p244 | The derivative of an inverse function | The learner can prove that if f is continuous and strictly monotone on an interval and differentiable at c with f'(c) nonzero, then the inverse is differentiable at d = f(c) with derivative 1/f'(c), and can explain why the inverse is not differentiable at d when f'(c) = 0 (as x^3 at 0). | needs: p232
  p245 | Derivatives of n-th roots and rational powers | The learner can use the inverse function theorem to prove (x^(1/n))' = (1/n) x^(1/n - 1) for x > 0, then the chain and power rules to differentiate x^(m/n), and can relate the failure at 0 to the vertical tangent of x^(1/3). | needs: p239, p243, p244
  p246 | x^2 sin(1/x): a derivative that is not continuous | The learner can show that f(x) = x^2 sin(1/x) with f(0) = 0 is differentiable everywhere, computing f'(0) = 0 from the definition with a squeeze argument and f'(x) = 2x sin(1/x) - cos(1/x) elsewhere with the rules, and prove that f' has no limit at 0, so a derivative need not be continuous. | needs: p238, p243

Piece: The mean value theorem and its consequences
Topic t18: The mean value theorem and its consequences
  p220 | Local extrema and Fermat's theorem | The learner can define local maxima and minima and prove that if a function has a local extremum at an interior point where it is differentiable, then the derivative there is zero. They can explain why endpoints and points of non-differentiability must be checked separately (|x| at 0, x on [0,1]), why f'(c) = 0 does not force an extremum (x^3), and how to find the extreme values of a continuous function on a closed interval. | needs: nothing
  p221 | Rolle's theorem | The learner can state and prove Rolle's theorem (f continuous on [a, b], differentiable on (a, b), f(a) = f(b) gives some c in (a, b) with f'(c) = 0) by combining the extreme value theorem with Fermat's theorem, and can show with counterexamples that each hypothesis is needed. | needs: p220
  p222 | The mean value theorem | The learner can state the mean value theorem with its exact hypotheses (continuity on the closed interval, differentiability on the open one), prove it from Rolle's theorem by subtracting the secant line, read it geometrically as a tangent parallel to the chord, and understand that c is only known to exist somewhere in (a, b). | needs: p221
  p223 | The Cauchy mean value theorem | The learner can state and prove the generalised mean value theorem (f(b) - f(a))g'(c) = (g(b) - g(a))f'(c) by applying Rolle's theorem to a suitable combination of f and g, explain why applying the ordinary mean value theorem to f and g separately fails (it gives two different points), and interpret it as the mean value theorem for a parametric curve. | needs: p221
  p224 | Zero derivative means constant | The learner can prove that a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant, and hence that two functions with equal derivatives on an interval differ by a constant, and can show the result fails on a set that is not an interval (for example a function equal to 0 on (0, 1) and 1 on (2, 3)). | needs: p222
  p225 | The sign of the derivative and monotonicity | The learner can prove that on an interval f' >= 0 everywhere holds exactly when f is increasing, that f' > 0 everywhere makes f strictly increasing, and that the converse of the strict version fails (x^3 at 0). They can use this to find the intervals where a function increases or decreases. | needs: p222
  p226 | What a positive derivative at a single point does and does not give | The learner can prove from the definition that f'(c) > 0 gives f(x) > f(c) for x slightly to the right of c and f(x) < f(c) slightly to the left, and can show with x + 2x^2 sin(1/x) at 0 that f still need not be increasing on any interval around c, so a pointwise sign condition is weaker than a sign condition on an interval. | needs: p225
  p227 | Bounded derivative gives a Lipschitz bound | The learner can prove that |f'| <= M on an interval implies |f(x) - f(y)| <= M|x - y|, conclude that f is uniformly continuous there, and apply it to examples such as |sin x - sin y| <= |x - y| and sqrt x on [1, infinity). They also see the converse direction: a differentiable Lipschitz function has a bounded derivative. | needs: p222
  p228 | Proving inequalities with the mean value theorem | The learner can prove inequalities such as sin x <= x for x >= 0, e^x >= 1 + x, ln(1 + x) <= x and Bernoulli's inequality (1 + x)^r >= 1 + rx for r >= 1 and x > -1, either by applying the mean value theorem on a well-chosen interval or by showing an auxiliary difference function is monotone and checking its value at one point. They can also derive two-sided estimates such as 1/(2 sqrt(n + 1)) < sqrt(n + 1) - sqrt(n) < 1/(2 sqrt(n)). | needs: p222, p225
  p229 | Counting and locating roots with Rolle's theorem | The learner can use Rolle's theorem to show that between two zeros of f lies a zero of f', so that if f' never vanishes then f has at most one root, and combine this with the intermediate value theorem to prove an equation such as x^5 + x - 1 = 0 has exactly one real root. They can bound the number of roots of a function by the number of roots of its derivative plus one (for example, a polynomial of degree n has at most n real roots, by induction). | needs: p221
  p230 | The limit of the derivative and one-sided derivatives | The learner can prove with the mean value theorem that if f is continuous at c, differentiable near c, and f'(x) tends to L as x tends to c, then f is differentiable at c with f'(c) = L (and the one-sided version). They can use this to check differentiability of piecewise-defined functions at the joining point, and see from x^2 sin(1/x) that the derivative can exist at c even when the limit of f'(x) does not. | needs: p222
  p231 | Darboux's theorem: derivatives have the intermediate value property | The learner can prove that if f is differentiable on [a, b] and lambda lies strictly between f'(a) and f'(b), then f'(c) = lambda for some c in (a, b), by finding an interior minimum or maximum of f(x) - lambda x and applying Fermat's theorem. They conclude that a derivative cannot have a jump discontinuity (so a step function is not a derivative), even though, as x^2 sin(1/x) shows, a derivative need not be continuous. | needs: p220, p226

Piece: L'Hopital's rule and Taylor's theorem
Topic t17: L'Hopital's rule and Taylor's theorem
  p207 | L'Hopital's rule for the 0/0 form | State L'Hopital's rule precisely for f(x)/g(x) as x tends to c when f and g both tend to 0, including the hypotheses that g' is nonzero near c and that lim f'/g' exists, and prove it from the Cauchy mean value theorem, first for one-sided limits and then for two-sided ones. | needs: nothing
  p208 | L'Hopital's rule for infinity/infinity and limits at infinity | Extend the rule to limits as x tends to plus or minus infinity (by substituting x = 1/t) and to the case where |g(x)| tends to infinity, with the infinity/infinity case either proved by an epsilon argument using the Cauchy mean value theorem on [x, y] or carefully stated, and apply it to standard growth comparisons such as (log x)/x^a and x^n/e^x. | needs: p207
  p209 | When L'Hopital's rule fails or gives no answer | Check the hypotheses before applying the rule, and recognise the standard failures: the form is not indeterminate, lim f'/g' does not exist although lim f/g does (as for (x + sin x)/x at infinity), g' vanishes near the point, or repeated application cycles without end (as for sqrt(x^2 + 1)/x). | needs: p208
  p210 | Higher derivatives, the classes C^n and C-infinity, and the Leibniz formula | Define the n-th derivative inductively, the classes C^n and C-infinity of n times continuously and infinitely differentiable functions, give examples showing the classes are distinct (such as x^k|x|), and state and prove by induction the Leibniz formula for the n-th derivative of a product. | needs: nothing
  p211 | Taylor polynomials | Construct the Taylor polynomial of degree n of f at a point a as the unique polynomial of degree at most n whose value and first n derivatives at a agree with those of f, and compute it for standard functions such as e^x, sin x, cos x, log(1 + x) and (1 + x)^r. | needs: p210
  p212 | Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder | State and prove that if f is n + 1 times differentiable on an interval then f(x) equals its Taylor polynomial of degree n at a plus f^(n+1)(c)(x - a)^(n+1)/(n + 1)! for some c between a and x, by applying Rolle's theorem (or the Cauchy mean value theorem repeatedly) to a suitable auxiliary function, and see the case n = 0 as the mean value theorem itself. | needs: p211
  p213 | Estimating errors with the Taylor remainder | Bound the Lagrange remainder by bounding f^(n+1) on the relevant interval, and use this to approximate numbers such as e, sin(0.1) or sqrt(1.1) to a stated accuracy, choosing n large enough and justifying every step of the bound. | needs: p212
  p214 | Proving inequalities with Taylor's theorem | Use the sign of the remainder term to prove inequalities such as e^x >= 1 + x + x^2/2 for x >= 0, cos x >= 1 - x^2/2 and x - x^3/6 <= sin x <= x for x >= 0, and to show that the Taylor polynomial approximates f to higher order, so that (f(x) - P_n(x))/(x - a)^n tends to 0. | needs: p212
  p215 | The second derivative test for local extrema | Prove that if f'(c) = 0 and f''(c) > 0 then f has a strict local minimum at c (and the analogue for f''(c) < 0), see why the test is silent when f''(c) = 0 using x^3 and x^4, and state the higher-order version using the first nonzero derivative at c. | needs: p210
  p216 | Convex functions defined by chords | Define a convex function on an interval by the condition that every chord lies on or above the graph, f(tx + (1 - t)y) <= tf(x) + (1 - t)f(y), recognise standard convex and concave examples, and relate concavity of f to convexity of -f. | needs: nothing
  p217 | The slope (three-chord) inequality | Prove that for a convex function and points x < y < z the slopes of the chords over [x, y], [x, z] and [y, z] increase in that order, and deduce that a convex function on an open interval has one-sided derivatives everywhere and is therefore continuous there. | needs: p216
  p218 | Convexity from the first and second derivatives | Prove that a differentiable function on an interval is convex exactly when its derivative is increasing, equivalently when its graph lies above every tangent line, and deduce that f'' >= 0 on the interval implies convexity, so that functions such as e^x, x^2 and -log x can be shown convex. | needs: p217
  p219 | Jensen's inequality (optional) | Prove by induction the finite form of Jensen's inequality f(sum t_i x_i) <= sum t_i f(x_i) for a convex f and weights t_i >= 0 summing to 1, and use it with the convexity of -log x to derive the inequality between the arithmetic and geometric means. | needs: p216
